# Exemplar corpus

**Warning, read before using anything here.**

Several pairs below involve real, named companies. Agora may quote these as **attributed structure**: what shape the copy took and why a practitioner judged it. Agora may never restate a real company's results as fact, may never reuse their copy, and may never imply a company endorses anything. Where a speaker asserts an outcome, the outcome is their claim and is unverified.

Lines marked `verbatim: yes` were quoted directly on screen or read aloud by the speaker. Lines marked `verbatim: reconstructed` are the speaker's paraphrase of copy they were describing, and should not be presented as a company's actual wording.

Fictional fixtures for the reference document must be built fresh. Do not port these into `Applied weak and strong pairs`; use them to derive the shape, then invent the facts, per the skill's own rule that examples teach structure and never facts.

## Hero and headline

### Specificity against superlative
Source: Peep Laja, Wynter B2B messaging course. verbatim: yes (both lines spoken as a contrasting pair)

> Weak: the fastest pizza delivery in town
> Strong: we deliver pizzas in 15 minutes

Why: the superlative is unverifiable and self-serving, so it reads as noise. The number is checkable and therefore credible. Speaker's framing: superlatives add to vagueness rather than reducing it.

### Generic benefit against quantified claim
Source: Peep Laja. verbatim: yes

> Weak: increase your sales
> Strong: average customer increases their pipeline value by [figure]

Why: every vendor in a category promises more money and less time, so the generic benefit does no discriminating work. Speaker attaches an explicit condition: the number has to be true.

### Category feature against situation
Source: Harry Dry, describing Loom's repositioning. real_company: Loom. verbatim: reconstructed

> Weak framing (what competitors said): an easier to use screen recorder
> Strong framing (what Loom said): the world has gone remote but remote communication is bad, so: async video messaging

Why: the weak version competes inside an existing product bucket on a comparative adjective. The strong version names a situation the buyer is living in and lets the category follow from it. Note this is the same move Agora's `Very short` guidance already prescribes, arriving from a practitioner rather than from doctrine.

### Category as orientation
Source: April Dunford, citing Snowflake's early positioning. real_company: Snowflake. verbatim: yes

> a data warehouse for the cloud

Why: works because the reader already owns "data warehouse". The category term is doing orientation work, not taxonomy retreat. This is the counterexample Agora needs so that it stops treating all category language as a failure.

### Concrete contrast between two named products
Source: Harry Dry, site teardown, proposing copy. real_company: Gong, Salesforce. verbatim: reconstructed, this is Dry's suggested rewrite and not Gong's live copy

> Salesforce tells you what happened. Gong tells you what was said.

Why: a comparison built on a single concrete distinction, expressed in two parallel short sentences with one word changing. Deliberate parallelism carrying the contrast.

### Number-led opening
Source: Harry Dry, describing Ramp copy. real_company: Ramp. verbatim: reconstructed

> Eight. That is how many businesses tried Ramp's corporate card in 2024 and decided [against it].

Why: the bare number as the first word creates a gap the next sentence closes. Depends entirely on the number being true and surprising.

### Two-line hook
Source: Harry Dry, crediting Dave Gerhardt and Joe Sugarman. verbatim: yes

> It takes 3.1 seconds to read this ad.
> The same time it takes a Model S to go from 0 to 60.

Why: the stated principle is that the first line's only job is to get the second line read. The specificity of 3.1 rather than 3 is the load-bearing detail.

### Alliteration plus juxtaposition
Source: Harry Dry, on Hinge. real_company: Hinge. verbatim: yes

> The dating app designed to be deleted.

Why: Dry's analysis is that the brief was something unmemorable about wanting people to find lasting relationships, and the writer's job was to make it memorable. Two devices carry it: alliteration on d, and the contradiction of a product that wants to be uninstalled.

### Cliché against opinion
Source: Harry Dry, site teardown. verbatim: yes

> Weak: good design takes brains and strategy
> Stronger: great design requires guts and taste

Why: Dry's objection to the first is not that it is false but that it is agreed. "Tell me stuff I don't know. Have a perspective beyond that." The second is arguable, and therefore says something.

### Vague adjective stacking
Source: Harry Dry, live teardown of a hiring platform. verbatim: yes (the criticized copy)

> Weak: the hiring platform that scales with your business ... security, reliability and scale you can count on

Why: Dry's diagnosis is missing detail rather than wrong claims: "which integrations, where, how many". Note his method, which is to paste the sentence into a search engine and see whether many companies use it unchanged.

### Wellness abstraction
Source: Harry Dry, live teardown. verbatim: yes (the criticized copy)

> Weak: elevating self-care ... your stylish dose of wellness

Why: "that is just an abstraction. What do you mean? Can we get specifics?" A worked instance of an abstract noun standing in for an explanation.

## Body copy

### Answer, do not ask
Source: Peep Laja. verbatim: reconstructed

> Weak subhead: why should you sign up with our company?
> Strong subhead: [the answer stated directly]

Why: speaker's rule is "don't ask questions, give answers". Recorded with the open conflict noted in `craft-findings.md`.

### Rhythm through deliberate repetition
Source: Harry Dry. verbatim: yes

> Raise twice the cash, hire twice the staff, spend twice as much on ads, cross both your fingers.

Why: Dry identifies the repeated "twice" and "both" as the rhythm carrier. Useful as evidence that parallelism is a craft tool, not only a machine-writing tell.

### Modest claim beats a big one
Source: Harry Dry, on his own headline. verbatim: yes

> increase conversions from 1% to 2%

Why: his stated reason for preferring this over a bigger promise is that it is not a big claim, and that "twice as fast" needed the arithmetic spelled out so nobody had to do it. Small, checkable, and it survives the falsifiability test.

### Before-and-after timeline
Source: Harry Dry, describing Ramp copy. real_company: Ramp. verbatim: reconstructed

> New software shouldn't take a year to implement. Here's what you can get done in 30 days. Today you can get started. On day five you've connected everything. On day 30 you're up, and you ask why you didn't switch years ago.

Why: Dry's note is that any B2B company could copy this section structurally, which he means as praise for the pattern rather than the copy. The structure converts an abstract objection about implementation cost into a dated sequence.

## CTA and microcopy

### Destination accuracy against approval state
Source: UX writing workshop teardown of a live training registration form. verbatim: yes

> Weak: I participate
> Strong: send request

Why: the form did not enroll the user, it submitted a request for approval. The weak label states an outcome the click does not produce. The single best demonstration in this corpus of the CTA-destination test.

### Over-narrow label
Source: same workshop. verbatim: yes

> Weak: ask about the product
> Strong: get in touch, or contact us

Why: the control was the site's general contact route, but the label admitted only product questions. Recorded as the failure at the opposite end from vagueness.

### Action framing on a trial
Source: split-test roundup, vendor-reported, outcome unverified. real_company: named in source as a travel brand. verbatim: yes

> Weak: sign up for free
> Strong: trial for free

Why: reported to have doubled conversions with design and offer held constant. Both are short verb phrases; the winner names what the reader does with the product rather than what they do with the form. Treat the figure as a prior, not a finding.

### Objection microcopy under a booking control
Source: Harry Dry, teardown. verbatim: reconstructed as a procedure rather than as copy

> Under the button, answer in sequence: what is the goal, how does it work, what will you walk away with, who is this for.
> Add an explicit low-commitment line where the ask is ambiguous, for example: there's no pressure to continue.

Why: Dry generates the list from the objections a reader holds at the moment of clicking, naming time cost, technical prerequisites, and privacy as the usual three.

## Positioning and profile

### Internal boredom is not a defect
Source: April Dunford. verbatim: yes (the internal objection she quotes)

> Internal objection: we've been saying this forever, it's so boring, everybody knows it already, there's nothing interesting here.

Why: her position is that this is a bad reason to change working copy, because the team sees the homepage daily and the buyer sees it once. Included as an anti-pattern in the reviewer rather than in the copy.

### Jargon boundary
Source: Peep Laja. verbatim: yes

> Justified: ABM software sold to buyers who are shopping for ABM software.
> Not justified: we're synergistically trying to energize the marketplace dynamics.

Why: the speaker's own test for the second class is whether it is a phrase you would use talking to a colleague, a sibling, or a parent.
