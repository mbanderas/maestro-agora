# Folklore ledger

The single block list. Every claim below is widely repeated in marketing, copywriting, UX, or plain-language practice and fails the evidence standard when stated as a rule. **Nothing here may become a rule in the skill.**

Merged and deduplicated from the folklore appendices of the six deep-research runs and the practitioner mining pass. Where two sources recorded the same myth from different lineages, the entry carries both. `research/folklore-ledger-yt.md` is superseded by this file and stays only as the raw record of the video pass.

A D grade means the claim arrived without method, sample, or source in the material reviewed. It does not mean the claim is false. Several are plausible and worth testing on a specific property; that status is a testable prior, not a rule, and the difference is the whole point of this file.

## How to use it

Before writing any rule that sounds familiar, search this file. If the rule is here, either it does not go in, or it goes in with the surviving narrow form stated in the `What survives` column and never with the popular form.

Three of these are already actively guarded inside the skill, in `Evidence register > Myths this document must never assert`. The rest are guarded only by this file.

## Headlines and titles

| Myth | Lineage | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| Five times as many people read the headline as read the body | Ogilvy, 1963, repeated ever since | Nothing usable. No sampling frame, readership study, medium definition, or denominator travels with it, and it cannot be transported into search, email, social, video, or web contexts. |
| Eight out of ten read the headline, two out of ten read the rest | Earliest dated web instance located is a 2006 blog formulation, amplified afterwards | Nothing. No primary study is cited anywhere in the chain. The memorable ratio is why it survived. |
| The headline is eighty percent of your advertising spend | Ogilvy again, derived directly from the claim above | Nothing. It is a rhetorical conversion of an unmeasured readership ratio into a budget metaphor. |
| The best headline length is six words | A 2014 aggregator citing a post citing unspecified usability research | Nothing, and current evidence contradicts it. Titles have no platform length limit, and greater length has sometimes correlated with higher response in randomized archives. |
| Search titles should be 50 to 60 characters | Tool and preview conventions built around historical desktop rendering widths | Front-load the distinguishing information. There is no title length limit, displayed titles are truncated to the device, and the engine can rewrite the title entirely. |
| Social headlines should be 70 characters | Early card-optimization guides written against layouts that have since changed | Make the first semantic unit intelligible without the final clause. No cross-client character cutoff is portable. |
| Odd numbers outperform even numbers | A vendor headline-analysis claim of a 20 percent advantage, propagated widely | Nothing. No primary randomized report, sample construction, or control for topic and list size survives in the repetitions. |
| Eight-word headlines get 21 percent more clicks | Same vendor cluster | Nothing. The percentage looks precise and hides the entire qualification burden. |
| A colon or hyphen improves response by 9 percent | Same vendor cluster | Nothing. Punctuation is confounded with two-part structure, topic, length, and style. |
| Use power words | Direct-response tradition, amplified by scoring tools | Wording effects are real and contextual. Model-based attempts to predict winners from headline language alone remain weak, so no lexical list is a rule. |
| Always write 25 headlines before choosing | Editorial production practice of one publisher | Generating many candidates and testing them is supported. Twenty-five is a production constraint, not an optimum. |
| Never repeat a syntactic headline template within one deliverable | No traced empirical origin; it was the working rule this project started from | Split by function. Parallel syntax is correct for same-class headings; template concentration is managed for attention-oriented ones under a stated governance default. |
| Questions in headlines increase engagement | Question-headline advice predates the web; the empirical anchor is two experiments in reader-addressed platform contexts | Scoped, the finding holds inside those tested contexts. As a cross-surface law it is folklore. See the open conflict below. |

## Reader vocabulary and plain language

| Myth | Lineage | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| Write at a grade 8 reading level | Readability formulas plus later public-information policy conventions | Readability scores are a coarse alarm, never a definition of comprehension. Grade 8 can be a defensible accessibility ceiling for broad public audiences and is wrong for expert, legal, technical, and regulated writing. |
| Never use jargon | Orwell, 1946, as the early plain-style formulation | Never use reader-unowned jargon without a downstream reason to teach it. Do use reader-owned jargon when it is the reader's shortest precise handle. |
| Readability scores predict conversion | Practitioner marketing material, not a founding study | Readability features carry predictive information in particular datasets, with some features positively and others negatively related, and the same index reversing sign across its own range. Use metrics to pick variants to test, never to pick the winner. |
| Customers do not care how it works, only what it does | The "sell the sizzle" sales tradition, 1937 | Lead with the problem or consequence, then name the mechanism when the mechanism changes a criterion the reader must evaluate. False for technical procurement, security, medicine, finance, regulated products, and dense categories. |
| At most one unfamiliar concept per sentence | This project's own earlier draft | Nothing as a limit. Vocabulary coverage impairs comprehension continuously, not as a step function. It survives only as a review trigger with no measured basis. |
| Two abstract nouns in a clause is one too many | This project's own earlier draft | Nothing. Controlled eye-tracking found no independent comprehension penalty for nominalization against recommended alternatives. Rewrite an abstraction when it conceals an actor, action, cause, or consequence the reader needs. |
| Nobody reads long copy anymore | Advertising folklore predating the web, rebutted in print by 2002 | Attention is top-heavy and readers do scroll when the page earns it. Length has no context-free optimum. |

## Calls to action and microcopy

| Myth | Lineage | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| Red buttons convert better | One 2011 vendor test reporting a 21 percent advantage over green on a single page | Nothing general. Contrast, palette, audience, and component salience all changed meaning in that test. |
| Never use the word Submit | A 2010 observational comparison across many landing pages plus a context-free choice exercise | Prefer consequence-specific labels on consumer forms. The comparison was not randomized, and `Submit` is correct where submitting is literally the task. |
| One CTA per page always outperforms multiple | An attention-ratio doctrine from 2013 landing-page practice | One campaign goal is a useful design doctrine. Choice-overload meta-analysis finds an average effect near zero with strong moderators, and repeated instances of one action are not multiple competing actions. |
| First person always beats second person on buttons | One 2013 test reporting roughly a 90 percent lift for `Start my free trial` | Nothing. The famous comparison did not include a pronounless arm, method reporting is thin, and no replication was located. Treat pronoun framing as a test variable. |
| The CTA must be above the fold | 2010 eyetracking reporting roughly 80 percent of viewing time above the fold, later measured near 57 percent | First-viewport content receives disproportionate attention. That is not a placement rule, and a below-fold treatment has been reported to win by a wide margin in a compound test. |
| Outcome copy always beats action copy | Early-2010s case-study practice | Immediate consequence clarity, not grammatical outcome supremacy. The test record contains direct counterexamples in both directions. |
| Every CTA must start with an action verb | No traceable controlled origin | A verb helps when it clarifies the task. `Explore` has a verb and says nothing; `Pricing` has none and communicates its destination perfectly. |
| Slogan-shaped labels always underperform action labels | No study treating them as an experimental class | Reject them for operational ambiguity, which is observable, rather than for a conversion penalty, which is not measured. |
| Repeat the CTA every N sections | Landing-page guidance with no controlled origin | Repetition can reduce navigation cost after new evidence arrives. No cadence is defensible. |
| Changing `sign up for free` to `trial for free` doubles conversions | One vendor split-test roundup | Nothing usable. No sample size, duration, traffic level, or significance. The direction is a plausible prior; the magnitude is not. |
| Matching the landing page headline to its ad beats a page-optimized headline by 115 percent | Same roundup | Message match is well supported as a principle. This number is not evidence for it. |
| A bigger, higher-contrast CTA lifts signups 25 percent | Same roundup | Nothing, and it is visual rather than copy, so it sits outside this skill's scope regardless. |

## Awareness, sophistication, and category

| Myth | Lineage | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| The five stages of awareness are a proven framework | A 1966 practitioner copywriting book | A durable practitioner segmentation heuristic. No controlled program establishes that readers partition into those five states, that the set is exhaustive, or that matched copy outperforms alternatives. |
| Market sophistication moves through five stages, each requiring its prescribed move | Same book, adjacent chapter | A competitive-message audit. The prescribed moves are practitioner inference, and different segments inside one market have different exposure histories. |
| Buyers are 70 percent through the purchase before contacting sales | Three different statistics collapsed into one: 57 percent before supplier contact, then 67 percent of the journey done digitally, then a retelling as 70 percent before sales | Nothing. The statements are not interchangeable, and current field research puts the figure in a different place again, varying by study, sample, and definition. |
| You must create a new category to win | A 2016 category-design book, plus retrospective case interpretation | Category framing has controlled cognitive evidence: the first plausible category label shapes how a genuinely new product is understood. Compulsory category invention does not. Independently corroborated as folklore by a positioning practitioner who calls deliberate category creation magical thinking. |

## Emotion, narrative, and behavioral levers

| Myth | Lineage | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| People buy on emotion and justify with logic | Attributed to a sales tradition whose actual teaching was that both matter | Nothing as behavioral science. No source measures temporal ordering, the share of emotionally caused purchases, or post-hoc rationalization rates. |
| Emotional ads outperform rational ads two to one | Effectiveness-case databases reporting proportions of cases with large business effects | The case-database finding is commercially interesting. It is not an experimental average treatment effect and does not mean emotional creative doubles anything. |
| Stories are 22 times more memorable than facts | A marketing presentation, then broad attribution to a cognitive psychologist whose work does not contain the figure | Narrative persuasion effects are real and modest, and direct comparisons have often favoured statistical evidence for beliefs and attitudes. |
| Losses are twice as powerful as gains | A loss-aversion parameter fitted to risky-choice data in 1992 | Reference-dependent evaluation occurs. Message-framing meta-analysis finds gain-versus-loss differences commonly tiny, null, or favouring gain framing. Do not default to loss framing. |
| 95 percent of purchase decisions are subconscious | A 2003 consumer book quoting a general cognitive-science rule of thumb about thought, emotion, and learning | Nothing. The retelling changed both the dependent variable and the evidentiary status. |
| Open loops make unfinished information more memorable | Interrupted-task memory studies from the 1920s | No general memory advantage survived meta-analysis. What survives better is a tendency to resume interrupted tasks. Use real, closable information gaps instead. |
| A named individual is more persuasive than statistics | The identifiable-victim literature | The pooled effect was small and preregistered replications failed to reproduce the key demonstrations. Particular cases can still move particular decisions. |
| Fear appeals backfire | Popular retelling of the danger-control literature | High threat with inadequate efficacy increases defensive responding. Meta-analytic net effects of fear appeals are positive on average. Neither half licenses manufactured dread. |
| Interviewing five to seven customers for fifteen to twenty minutes is sufficient | A practitioner working heuristic presented as a threshold | Qualitative research on real customers is valuable. The numbers have no stated basis. |
| Emotional targeting produces sustained gains where element testing plateaus | Agency self-report across unnamed clients | The underlying claim that qualitative research beats element tweaking is plausible and unevidenced in this source. |

## Rhythm, style, voice, and detection

| Myth | Lineage | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| Vary your sentence length | A 1985 craft book chapter | Useful craft advice, incomplete as a specification. It supplies no target variance, no interaction with clause depth, and no boundary for intentional repetition. |
| Write like you talk | The same book, plus a widely read 2015 essay | Reject literally. Speech contains repairs, deixis, and prosody that writing removes or represents differently. The usable version is to preserve the author's natural directness and formality. |
| Average sentence length should be under 20 words | Twentieth-century readability formulas converted into prescriptions | Reject as a universal ceiling. Formulas establish associations between surface length and difficulty, not an optimal cadence. |
| Short, choppy sentences are what you want, and one-sentence paragraphs are ideal | Conversion-copywriting practice | Recorded as one side of an open conflict. See below. |
| Commas signal a sentence that should be broken apart | Same source | A useful editing prompt, never a rule. |
| Read your copy aloud in a false accent | Practitioner advice, source unremembered by the speaker | Plain read-aloud improves error detection in controlled proofreading experiments. The accent variant is not evidenced. |
| Passive voice is bad | Early twentieth-century style advice, hardened by later editions | Reject categorically. Passive voice changes information structure and agent prominence and is correct where the patient or result is the topic. Measure passive rate by register. |
| A self-contained quotable passage is two or three sentences | No primary source; the nearest antecedent is one retrieval configuration that happened to prefer three-sentence windows | Reject. The same study found another system best at six sentences, and self-containment is a referential and semantic property, not a sentence count. |
| AI detectors work by measuring perplexity and burstiness | A detector vendor's own 2023 public explanation, which that vendor has since superseded | Historically true of some simple detectors and false as a general explanation. Detector reliability is strongly domain-dependent, degrades under obfuscation, and shows severe false-positive bias against non-native English writing. |
| Never write an ad a competitor could sign | Attributed practitioner maxim | The maxim is unmeasured. Its operational form is checkable and was adopted: paste the line into a search engine and count the unrelated companies publishing it unchanged. |
| You can't talk, you can only point | Attributed to an unrecoverable source | The maxim is unmeasured. The falsifiability test it produces is operational and was adopted as a procedure. |
| The first line's only job is to get the second line read | Direct-response tradition | No measurement anywhere in the chain. |
| You can't parody honesty | Practitioner assertion | Unmeasurable. Kept as an articulation of why truth discipline and craft point the same way, never as a rule. |
| Superlatives destroy credibility | Practitioner assertion | The preference for a checkable specific over an unsupported superlative rests on this skill's own truth rules and on claim-skepticism evidence. The strong causal form is the speaker's and is not asserted. |
| Ideas arrive by incubation: work the problem, then forget it | Practitioner assertion invoking a fictional character | Incubation has a real research literature that the telling does not engage. |

## Conflicts left open

Neither side of either conflict may be written as a rule. Both are recorded inside the skill.

| Conflict | Position A | Position B | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question-form subheadings | Practitioners reject them: give the answer instead of asking. | Question headings were directly observed reading better than abstract noun-stack statement headings on the same page, and two experiments support question headlines in reader-addressed platform contexts. | Open. Untested hypothesis: questions beat noun stacks and lose to concrete answers. |
| Sentence cadence | Deliberate variance, with spread targets. | Uniform brevity on scanned commercial surfaces. | Open, probably register-dependent. Neither side is graded above an engineering heuristic. |

## Audit of the rules added in this hardening pass

Every rule added in phases 1 through 4 was checked against the list above. Findings:

- **No blocked claim was written as a rule.** The three procedures adopted from blocked maxims (search-paste, falsifiability, objection microcopy) were adopted as procedures at HOUSE grade with the D-grade origin named in the same paragraph and no effect on response claimed.
- **Specificity against superlative** was written without the blocked causal claim. The text says explicitly not to assert that superlatives destroy credibility or that specifics convert better.
- **The staging routing table** was written entirely at practitioner-inference grade, with the blocked five-stage-framework claim refused in the same section that uses the stages.
- **The emotion section** lists seven blocked levers by name as refused, which is the strongest available guard against reintroduction.
- **The prosody targets** are labelled governance defaults in every row, and the blocked under-20-words rule does not appear.
- **The voice reference** takes its corpus thresholds from sourced sample-size research and labels every other number a governance default. The blocked detector explanation is refused explicitly.

Two contradictions inside the existing document were found by this audit and fixed rather than recorded:

- `Conflict hierarchy` and `Written GEO/AEO and citability` both asserted that a self-contained passage is two or three sentences, while `Required comprehension tests` and the `Evidence register` had already refuted that as folklore. The two assertions were removed; the semantic test now governs both places.
- The deterministic invariant for heading-template runs carried a number with no label. It now says it is a governance default.

## Method note

Absence of evidence in a source is not evidence of absence. A D grade here means the claim arrived unsupported in the material reviewed, not that the claim is false, and several of these practitioners publish better-supported material elsewhere. The correct response to a D-graded claim that sounds right is to test it on the property in question, record the result with its method, and only then consider promoting it.
