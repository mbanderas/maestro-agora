# Emotional hooks, narrative transportation, motivation

## Evidence-graded craft reference under a truth constraint

### Discrete emotions and appraisal tendencies

**Assumption:** "Marketing context" here includes paid advertising, landing pages, sales pages, product pages, lifecycle messaging, and other commercial persuasion. Evidence from health, public-interest, laboratory, and donation contexts is treated as mechanism evidence, not automatically as proof of commercial conversion effects.

**Rule [A]: Treat discrete emotions as different decision states, not as points on a positive-negative scale.** Fear and anger are the canonical example. Both are negative and high-arousal, yet appraisal-tendency research found fear associated with lower certainty and control, higher perceived risk, and more precaution, while anger was associated with greater certainty and control, lower perceived risk, and less precaution. A nationally representative post-September 11 study likewise found fear and anger predicting opposite risk estimates and precautionary plans. A 2025 preregistered close replication with N = 780 reproduced the broader fear-versus-anger difference for general, positive, and ambiguous risk optimism, but did **not** reproduce the original effects on risk preference or risk optimism for negative events. The correct replication verdict is therefore "appraisal-dependent judgment effect partly replicated; broad risk-taking claim not replicated." There is no defensible universal standardized effect size for "fear versus anger on behavior." The often-repeated claim that anger makes people risk-seeking and fear makes them risk-averse is stronger than the current replication record supports.

**Boundary:** Do not infer that an emotion will produce the same behavior across domains. Appraisal tendencies alter judgments when the decision recruits the emotion's characteristic appraisal dimensions. They are not fixed action commands. The 2025 registered replication is direct evidence for that limitation.

The table's core craft conclusion is not that every message needs a target emotion. It is that **valence is an inadequate targeting variable**. Fear and anger can point decisions in opposite directions; sadness and disgust can produce opposite valuation patterns despite both being negative.

**Rule [A]: Match the emotional mechanism to the appraisal already justified by the facts. Do not select an emotion first and then search for facts that can be dramatized into it.** This follows directly from appraisal-tendency evidence showing that emotion-specific effects depend on appraisals such as certainty, control, responsibility, and threat rather than simple positive or negative tone.

**Boundary:** Incidental emotion, audience state, prior knowledge, and decision domain can alter or erase the expected effect. The 2025 registered replication shows that even a landmark appraisal-tendency result does not generalize cleanly from risk estimates to risk preferences.

### Fear and threat appeals

Fear appeals are one of the few emotional persuasion families with a mature meta-analytic literature. The popular version is still wrong in two important ways. First, fear appeals are not generally demonstrated to "backfire." Second, efficacy matters, but the modern evidence does not support the simplistic requirement that every effective appeal must contain both an explicit self-efficacy claim and an explicit response-efficacy claim.

Tannenbaum et al.'s 2015 meta-analysis synthesized 127 articles, 248 independent samples, and 27,372 participants. The average effect on the composite of attitudes, intentions, and behaviors was **d = 0.29** in the intended direction. Effects were stronger when messages included efficacy statements, depicted high susceptibility and severity, recommended one-time rather than repeated behavior, and included a larger proportion of women. The authors found very few circumstances producing negative average effects and found no general evidence that fear appeals made outcomes worse than control conditions.

A later meta-analysis by Bigsby and Albarracin covered **k = 158, N = 19,736**. Fear appeals produced stronger behavioral outcomes when they contained **positive response-efficacy information**, meaning information that the recommended response can actually address the danger. Presence of self-efficacy information did not independently moderate effectiveness in the same way, nor did negative response-efficacy information. That result qualifies the common prescription that both efficacy components must always be explicitly stated.

Earlier Witte and Allen meta-analytic work found the theoretically important danger-control pattern: strong threat paired with high efficacy produced the greatest adaptive change, while strong threat paired with low efficacy produced the greatest defensive responses such as avoidance or reactance. That is evidence for defensive processing, not evidence that fear appeals routinely create a negative net behavioral effect.

**Rule [A]: A threat appeal should contain an evidenced protective response whenever the copy asks the reader to act against the threat.** Positive response-efficacy information strengthens behavioral effects, and high-threat/low-efficacy combinations are the configuration most associated with defensive responding.

**Boundary:** Explicit self-efficacy copy is not independently required in every message. Bigsby and Albarracin did not find behavioral effects varying simply with the presence versus absence of self-efficacy information. Also, efficacy cannot rescue a threat that is irrelevant or fabricated.

**Rule [A]: Do not write "fear backfires" as a general rule. Write the narrower rule: high threat with inadequate efficacy can increase defensive processing, while meta-analytic net effects of fear appeals are positive on average.**

**Boundary:** A positive average does not license extreme fear. It does not establish ethical acceptability, audience relevance, or truth. It also does not show that increasing fear indefinitely increases persuasion.

For a truth-constrained system, a threat appeal is legitimate only after passing the following factual gate. This is a **system constraint**, not an empirical claim:

1. **The hazard exists.** There must be supplied evidence for the event, failure mode, loss, penalty, exposure, or adverse condition.
2. **Severity is evidenced.** The system may describe only consequences supported by the evidence. "Could delay the run by one day" cannot become "could jeopardize the quarter."
3. **Applicability is evidenced or made conditional.** If the system does not know whether a reader is exposed, it must write "If your team does X..." rather than "Your team is at risk."
4. **Probability is not invented.** A possible event may be called possible. It may not be called likely, common, imminent, growing, or inevitable without evidence for those attributes.
5. **The recommended action is real.** The product or action must actually perform the protective function described.
6. **Efficacy magnitude is not invented.** Knowing that a control blocks a class of access is different from knowing that it cuts incidents by 73 percent.
7. **Urgency is real.** Deadlines, stock limits, compliance dates, exposure windows, contract expirations, and time-to-failure must come from the supplied facts.
8. **The message provides a way forward.** A threat with no available response is ordinarily a poor persuasion structure and creates the high-threat/low-efficacy configuration associated with defensive responding.

A permissible fictional example illustrates the distinction. Assume the supplied facts for fictional company "Kestrel Archive" say: backups run once each night at 02:00; files created after 02:00 are not included until the following night's backup; no continuous replication is provided.

> "A file created at 9:00 a.m. is not in Kestrel Archive's backup until 2:00 a.m. the next day. If losing that day's work is material to your team, the nightly schedule is a constraint to account for."

This names the actual exposure. It does not invent an outage, estimate its probability, claim that customers have suffered losses, or tell the reader to be afraid.

Excluded tactics include fabricated danger, fabricated prevalence, fabricated deadlines, exaggerated consequences, unsupported "cost of doing nothing" calculations, invented incidents, invented regulator attention, fabricated cyberattacks, fake countdowns, and claims that a product prevents an outcome when the evidence establishes only that it provides a feature related to that outcome.

### Narrative transportation, identification, specificity, and vividness

Narrative transportation is not a synonym for "telling a story." Green and Brock defined it as absorption in a narrative involving **attention, imagery, and affect**. Their original research developed and validated the 15-item Transportation Scale and showed that greater transportation predicted more story-consistent beliefs and more favorable evaluations of protagonists. A later six-item short form was developed for more economical measurement.

**Rule [A]: Use narrative when the facts form a causally coherent sequence that a reader can mentally simulate. Do not convert a list of claims into a fictional customer journey merely to obtain story structure.** Transportation is strongest when attention, imagery, and affect can cohere around events rather than a disconnected sequence of assertions. The consumer transportation literature includes a meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes from 76 published and unpublished articles.

**Boundary:** Transportation can also make false or fictional propositions influential. Green and Brock found story-consistent persuasion was not simply abolished by labeling a narrative fiction. For a truth-constrained system, that makes narrative a higher, not lower, evidence-risk format. Every event represented as real must be real.

Narrative persuasion effects are real but modest. In health communication, Shen, Sheer, and Li synthesized 25 studies with **N = 9,330** and reported an overall effect around **r = .063**. Effects varied by medium and behavioral target; audio/video narratives showed stronger effects than print in that synthesis, and prevention/detection outcomes behaved differently from cessation.

That effect size is materially different from marketing folklore suggesting that stories overwhelm facts.

Direct comparisons of anecdotal and statistical evidence are particularly damaging to "story always wins." Allen and Preiss's earlier meta-analysis of 15 investigations found **statistical evidence more persuasive than narrative evidence, r = .101**. A larger 2020 meta-analysis of 61 papers likewise found statistical evidence stronger overall for beliefs and attitudes, while anecdotes could have an advantage for intentions and under conditions of high emotional engagement or personal relevance.

**Rule [A]: Do not replace quantitative evidence with a customer story when the decision turns on the magnitude, frequency, probability, or generality of an effect.** Statistical evidence has the stronger record for beliefs and attitudes in direct evidence-type comparisons.

**Boundary:** Anecdotal evidence can be more influential for intentions and in high-emotion or personally relevant conditions. That does not make the anecdote representative. A single true customer experience remains one case.

This yields the correct answer to "When does a customer story outperform a claim?": **there is no general rule that it does.** A true customer narrative can outperform abstract evidence on some intention outcomes or by increasing engagement and identification; statistical evidence can outperform it on belief and attitude outcomes. A customer story should therefore do experiential work that aggregate evidence cannot do, while aggregate claims should carry generality and magnitude.

**Rule [B]: Identification is safest when it is built from real shared circumstances, roles, constraints, or goals rather than an asserted inner similarity.** Identification research indicates that perceived similarity can facilitate character identification and narrative engagement, but similarity is not necessary in every case and can be disrupted by contradictory personal experience.

**Boundary:** "Someone exactly like you" is not warranted merely because the customer shares a job title. Differences in prior experience, identity, values, and knowledge can disrupt identification. Under the hard truth constraint, the system may state a shared factual circumstance, not a presumed emotional identity.

The transfer from public-interest narrative research to commercial copy must remain explicit. Health and public-interest communication has accumulated randomized studies and multiple meta-analyses directly comparing narrative and nonnarrative messages. Consumer research also has a mature narrative-transportation literature, but much of it measures transportation, attitudes, intentions, or brand responses rather than long-run sales or profit. It is therefore legitimate to transfer the **mechanism** of transportation to commercial writing, but not the effect size from a health intervention to a sales page.

#### Specificity and vividness

Vividness has a stronger empirical basis than many copywriting treatments imply, but it is not "add sensory adjectives." Blondé and Girandola's meta-analysis covered 27 articles, 43 effects, and **N = 7,575**. Vivid communication produced **d = .31** for attitudes and **d = .39** for behavioral intentions. Heterogeneity was substantial, so the average masks meaningful variation.

**Rule [A]: Make the decision-relevant mechanism vivid, not the decoration around it.** Vividness can increase persuasive impact, but its value lies in making the represented consequence, process, or situation easier to encode and mentally represent.

**Boundary:** Vividness effects are heterogeneous and can distract attention from the argument. More concrete detail is not monotonically better. A vivid but irrelevant office description, founder anecdote, or scenic setup can consume attention without improving the decision.

For truth-constrained copy, "vivid" means increasing resolution **inside the evidence**:

Flat:

> "The importer reports schema errors."

Vivid, using only supplied fictional facts:

> "The upload stops at the first invalid header and returns 'schema error.' It does not name the column."

The second version adds no event, customer, feeling, frequency, or damage claim. It exposes the operational texture already present in the fact set.

#### The identifiable-victim effect

The identifiable-victim effect is a warning against importing famous behavioral findings without checking their replication status. Lee and Feeley's meta-analysis of 41 effects reported only a **small overall effect, r = .05**.

A later robust Bayesian reanalysis found evidence consistent with publication bias. The three highest-powered studies were near zero, including a study with **N = 12,802 and r = .004**. Maier and colleagues' preregistered far replication reported no empirical support for the focal effect, with estimates essentially at zero. A separate 2024 preregistered replication of the "single identified victim" result likewise found **partial eta squared = .00** for the focal comparison and failed to reproduce the original interaction.

**Rule [A]: Do not use "one named person is more persuasive than statistics" as a general craft law.** The original meta-analytic effect was tiny and later preregistered replication work failed to reproduce key demonstrations.

**Boundary:** Some identifiable cases can still evoke emotion and aid helping decisions. The replication record rejects the universal lever, not the possibility of effects in particular conditions.

Under the truth constraint, an invented "representative customer," composite testimonial presented as a real person, fabricated name, fake photograph, or fictional victim represented as factual is excluded. A disclosed fictional scenario may be used for explanation, but it cannot function as social evidence.

### Loss framing, endowment, curiosity, and open loops

#### Loss framing

Prospect theory does not establish the copywriting rule "frame the offer as a loss because losses are stronger than gains." Prospect theory models choices around reference points under specified risk conditions. Message-framing research asks a different question: whether logically related persuasive messages perform differently when consequences are phrased as gains from compliance or losses from noncompliance.

The meta-analytic framing record is much less dramatic than the popular marketing version. O'Keefe and Jensen's prevention-message meta-analysis found **no significant overall persuasive advantage** for loss framing. Their work on message engagement found a slight advantage for **gain-framed** messages rather than loss-framed messages. A later synthesis cited an effect around **r = .032** in favor of gain framing, which is tiny. Health-message meta-analysis has also found a small gain-frame advantage for some prevention behaviors, around **r = .083**, while attitudes, intentions, detection behaviors, and individual domains varied.

Commercial evidence does not rescue the popular rule. A 2025 advertising study found loss-framed product descriptions attracted more attention but produced **lower purchase intention** than gain-framed descriptions. A 2026 controlled advertising study found gain framing produced more favorable attitudes with rational appeals and positive emotional appeals, while negative emotional appeals showed no gain-loss difference. These are individual studies, not grounds for another universal rule.

**Rule [A]: Do not default to loss framing. Test gain and loss formulations when both are factually equivalent; otherwise use the frame that describes the real decision most directly.** Meta-analytic effects are small, variable, and frequently null, with some evidence favoring gain framing.

**Boundary:** Framing can interact with behavioral domain, perceived risk, prevention versus detection, involvement, emotional tone, and prior knowledge. A null or gain-frame average does not mean a loss frame can never win in a specific application.

For a hard-truth system, a loss frame is permitted only when the "loss" is actually entailed by the facts. "Keep $400 that would otherwise be charged in September" can be legitimate if the charge and amount are real. "Stop losing thousands every month" is excluded without evidence establishing both the loss and magnitude.

#### Endowment and ownership

Ownership effects are real but are not equivalent to generic loss aversion. A preregistered meta-analysis of 26 samples with **N = 3,024** estimated the mere-ownership effect on valuation at approximately **Hedges' g = 0.57, 95% CI [0.46, 0.69]**. The authors also emphasized that mere ownership is routinely conflated with the endowment effect and mere exposure.

Consumer research further shows that the ownership effect can arise partly because a possession becomes associated with the self, and that identity relevance and self-threat alter the effect. Other research shows willingness-to-accept versus willingness-to-pay gaps can also depend on beliefs about market prices, complicating a simple "ownership causes loss aversion" explanation.

**Rule [A]: Ownership language is evidence-based only when ownership, control, allocation, or possession is real.** Mere ownership can raise valuation, but the mechanism depends on an actual or psychologically credible ownership relation.

**Boundary:** Effects vary with self-association, identity relevance, context, and market beliefs. The g = 0.57 estimate is not a predicted lift from saying "your" in a headline.

Excluded uses include "your savings," "your new revenue," "your customers are waiting," or "the growth you are leaving behind" when those possessions, outcomes, audiences, or losses have not been established. Grammatical possession is not psychological ownership.

#### Curiosity and information gaps

Loewenstein's information-gap theory describes curiosity as arising when attention is focused on a discrepancy between what a person knows and what the person wants to know. The important element is not arbitrary withholding. The reader must have enough structure to perceive a specific, potentially closable information gap.

Experimental neuroscience gives curiosity genuine motivational status. Gruber, Gelman, and Ranganath found that high-curiosity states improved memory for the sought answers and also improved memory for incidental material encountered during the curious state, both immediately and after a one-day delay. Curiosity engaged neural systems associated with reward and memory. This establishes curiosity as more than a metaphor for attention, but it does not establish a universal conversion lift from teaser headlines.

**Rule [B]: Open a specific information gap only when the copy possesses a real answer and can close the gap within the promised scope.** The evidence supports curiosity from perceived information gaps and improved learning under curiosity. It does not validate arbitrary withholding.

**Boundary:** A gap that is too broad, irrelevant, already known, or impossible to close need not generate useful curiosity. Curiosity depends on prior knowledge and perceived value of the missing information.

The "open loop" literature is badly distorted by the Zeigarnik effect. The classic claim is that unfinished tasks are remembered better than completed tasks. A 2025 meta-analysis found **no overall memory advantage for unfinished tasks**. What survived more clearly was the Ovsiankina effect, a tendency to **resume** interrupted tasks. The modern evidence therefore supports "interruption can create resumption pressure under some conditions" better than "open loops make content memorable."

**Rule [A]: Do not justify teaser copy with "the Zeigarnik effect makes unfinished things memorable."** The modern meta-analysis did not find the predicted memory advantage.

**Boundary:** Interrupted tasks can still create a tendency to resume, and effects depend on involvement, situational demands, individual differences, and interruption conditions.

Direct controlled evidence for a general numerical **trust-decay effect** caused by unresolved marketing "open loops" is not established in this evidence base. Treat payoff failure as a truth-contract problem, not as a quantified psychological law. A headline that promises "the three permissions causing the error" must deliver three evidenced permissions. A teaser that implies hidden evidence and then supplies none has made a misleading promise regardless of whether an experiment has measured the exact trust decrement.

### Social proof, identity, self-signaling, and in-groups

Social proof is not one mechanism. At minimum, distinguish **descriptive norms**, what others do, from **injunctive norms**, what others approve or disapprove.

A consumer-decision meta-analysis covering **200 independent studies and 659 effect sizes** found descriptive norms exerted stronger effects on behavior, while injunctive norms exerted stronger effects on attitudes. Behavioral effects were stronger when the norm came from a close, concrete source and when the behavior was public rather than private. A later meta-analysis across 297 studies likewise found distinct pathways for descriptive and injunctive norms.

**Rule [A]: Use a descriptive norm only when the behavior, reference group, denominator, measurement period, and direction of the norm are actually known.** Descriptive norms can affect behavior, but their effect depends materially on the source and behavioral context.

**Boundary:** A descriptive norm can move behavior in the **wrong direction**. Schultz et al.'s field experiment found that telling households the neighborhood average reduced energy consumption among high users but increased consumption among households already below the average. Adding an injunctive approval signal eliminated that boomerang effect.

This is one of the most important reversals in practical persuasion.

Suppose fictional "Northbank Compute" knows from audited logs that 72 percent of teams have enabled hardware-key authentication. "72% of Northbank teams have hardware-key authentication enabled" is a factual descriptive norm. It may be useful for a nonadopting audience.

Suppose instead that 72 percent **have not** enabled it. Writing "Many teams are still running without hardware keys" may normalize the undesired behavior. The social-proof lever has reversed.

**Rule [A]: Never advertise the prevalence of an undesirable behavior merely to establish that the problem is common.** Descriptive norms can normalize the behavior being discouraged.

**Boundary:** A message can sometimes neutralize the boomerang with a credible injunctive norm, but the approval norm itself must be evidenced. A fake badge, fake "recommended" label, or invented consensus is excluded.

Fabrication-dependent social-proof tactics excluded from the consuming system include fake customer counts, fake download counts, fabricated waiting lists, fake scarcity produced by "X people viewing now," fabricated review averages, invented market leadership, cherry-picked prevalence presented as population prevalence, fake testimonials, undisclosed employee reviews, and "teams like yours choose..." without a measured reference group.

#### Identity and self-signaling

Identity effects are real, but social proof and identity can pull in opposite directions. Escalas and Bettman's experiments found stronger self-brand connections when brand images were consistent with an in-group; out-group associations could weaken connection, especially among more independently self-construing consumers. Effects were stronger for more symbolic brands.

Berger and Heath showed the reversal directly: consumers sometimes **diverge** from majorities or out-groups when the product domain is identity-signaling. Four studies found divergence particularly in domains treated as symbolic of identity. A product being popular can therefore make it less attractive to someone who uses the product category to signal distinction.

**Rule [A]: In-group framing works best when the group membership is real and the product or behavior genuinely carries group-relevant meaning.**

**Boundary:** In identity-signaling domains, majority adoption or out-group adoption can trigger divergence rather than conformity. Identity effects are weaker when the product is not symbolically diagnostic.

**Rule [A]: Do not combine "everyone is doing it" with identity signaling as though they are additive levers.** In symbolic domains, widespread adoption can undermine distinctiveness.

**Boundary:** For private, functional, low-symbolism decisions, descriptive norms can operate more straightforwardly. For public or symbolic decisions, group meaning becomes more important.

Permissible identity framing:

> "Built for payroll administrators who need the export before the bank cutoff."

This refers to a real role and a real constraint if both are supplied facts.

Excluded:

> "For operators who refuse to settle."

The sentence assigns a value, attitude, and self-concept that the evidence does not establish.

Also excluded are fabricated tribes, invented customer identities, unsupported "people like you" statements, invented professional consensus, fake status hierarchies, and claims that ownership signals taste, intelligence, seriousness, courage, expertise, rebellion, or sophistication unless those associations are themselves evidenced.

### Emotion from true material only

**Assumption:** The available fact set in this section may contain product mechanics, workflow steps, interfaces, limits, permissions, prices, dates, contract terms, error states, constraints, and reversibility conditions. It contains **no outcome data, no testimonials, no customer quotations, no prevalence claims, no market claims, and no evidence of what users feel**.

That fact set is still sufficient for emotional writing. The system must stop treating emotion as an adjective layer placed over evidence. The reliable route is to expose the humanly consequential structure already inside the facts.

#### Felt texture of a real workflow failure

**Procedure [A]: Convert an abstract failure state into the smallest concrete sequence of true operations that makes the failure mentally simulable.** This is the most defensible application of the vividness literature, which finds small-to-medium effects on attitudes and intentions, **d = .31 and d = .39**, while warning through substantial heterogeneity that vividness is not universally helpful.

**Boundary:** The procedure fails when concrete detail is irrelevant, decorative, or not supported by the fact set. Do not invent an employee, late night, deadline, customer reaction, missed meeting, bodily sensation, emotional label, or downstream consequence.

Drafting sequence:

1. Find the exact point where the workflow stops, branches, repeats, or loses information.
2. List only actions and states explicitly supported by the supplied facts.
3. Replace category words such as "inefficient," "friction," or "manual work" with those operations.
4. Stop before adding an unevidenced consequence.
5. Let the reader supply the emotional judgment.

Fictional supplied facts for "Morrow Import":

- CSV uploads require eight named headers.
- The import stops when any required header is missing.
- The error message reads "Schema invalid."
- The error does not identify the missing header.
- The documented recovery procedure is to compare the uploaded headers with the eight-header specification and upload again.

Mechanically accurate but inert:

> "Morrow Import validates CSV schemas and reports invalid files."

Truthful emotional rendering:

> "The upload stops at 'Schema invalid.' It does not name the header. The recovery step is to compare the file against all eight required headers, correct the mismatch, and upload again."

Nothing says "frustrating." Nothing needs to. The sequence contains the friction.

Impermissible:

> "Another twenty minutes wasted hunting for a typo."

There is no evidence for twenty minutes, for waste, or for a typo.

Impermissible:

> "You know the sinking feeling."

The fact set contains no evidence about feeling.

#### Recognition through a precisely named situation

Recognition is the response produced when a reader can map copy onto an experience or condition already represented in the facts. It is safer than telling the reader what they want because it operates on observable circumstances rather than inferred motives.

**Procedure [B]: Name the exact state transition, timing condition, role conflict, or edge case that defines the problem; do not name the reader's emotion.** Narrative identification research supports the importance of perceived similarity and relevant personal experience, while vividness research supports concrete representation. The full copy procedure itself has not been separately validated as a commercial intervention, so the grade remains B.

**Boundary:** Recognition collapses when the situation is too generic or when the writer adds a supposedly universal reaction. "When a request changes after approval" is a situation. "When a last-minute change ruins your afternoon" invents both timing significance and emotion.

Fictional supplied facts for "Alder Approvals":

- A request can be edited while status = Draft.
- Once submitted, normal editors cannot change it.
- An administrator can return a submitted request to Draft.
- The administrator action must occur before the editor can modify the request.

Flat:

> "Submitted requests require administrator intervention to edit."

Recognition-led:

> "The request is editable until you submit it. Change one line afterward, and an administrator has to return it to Draft before you can edit that line."

The second version uses a concrete moment of recognition without claiming that this happens often, that users hate it, or that it causes delay.

**Drafting test:** Delete all emotion words. If the passage becomes emotionally dead, the copy was depending on emotional assertion rather than situation recognition.

#### Relief through resolved ambiguity

Relief does not require a sentence that says "finally, peace of mind." A copy system can create the structural conditions for relief by opening a real uncertainty and then resolving it with exact information.

Curiosity research supports information gaps as motivational states, and threat-avoidance research supports relief as a distinct response to successful resolution or avoidance.

**Procedure [B]: Put the ambiguity immediately next to its verified resolution.** The emotional work comes from the transition from "unknown" to "known," not from adding a relief adjective.

**Boundary:** This does not work when the resolution is incomplete, delayed beyond the promised scope, or merely reassuring language without diagnostic information. It also fails when the copy invents an ambiguity the user did not have.

Fictional supplied facts for "Sable Tax":

- Validation occurs before submission.
- An invalid row is returned with the row number and field name.
- A submission with validation errors is not transmitted.
- After correcting the named field, the user can validate again.

Flat:

> "Sable Tax provides field-level validation."

Resolution-led:

> "Before anything is submitted, validation names the row and field that failed. 'Row 184: VAT country missing.' Fix that field and run validation again."

The second version does not claim "peace of mind," "confidence," "no more guesswork," or "stress-free filing." It performs the informational resolution that could generate relief.

**Rule [A]: Prefer actual closure to the word "relief."** Open-loop evidence does not support withholding information merely to create tension, while curiosity evidence supports valued, answerable gaps.

**Boundary:** Some long-form narratives legitimately defer resolution. In task copy, withholding an available answer usually works against the user's immediate informational goal.

#### Agency through a reversible decision

Agency can be communicated without claiming that the reader wants control. State the options and the verified ability to reverse, stop, edit, cancel, restore, or defer.

Fear-appeal research provides strong evidence that perceived efficacy changes how threat information is processed, although it does **not** establish a universal "reversibility conversion effect." The craft procedure is therefore deliberately narrower than the popular claim.

**Procedure [B]: When a choice is genuinely reversible, state the reversible action, the point until which it remains available, and any real cost of reversal.** This provides concrete control information without inventing a desire for control or promising reduced anxiety.

**Boundary:** Reversibility must be operationally true. Do not call something "risk-free," "no commitment," or "cancel anytime" if there are fees, notice periods, irreversible setup costs, data-loss consequences, minimum terms, or other qualifications.

Fictional supplied facts for "Fallow Metrics":

- Monthly subscriptions have no minimum term.
- A customer may change tier before the next renewal.
- Cancellation takes effect at the next renewal.
- There is no cancellation fee.
- Fees already paid for the current month are not refunded.

Weak emotional overlay:

> "Try Fallow with total confidence and zero risk."

Unsupported. "Total confidence" is an invented feeling. "Zero risk" ignores the nonrefundable current month and every other possible risk.

Agency-led:

> "Choose a monthly tier. Change tiers or cancel before the next renewal. There is no cancellation fee; the current month is not refunded."

The second version supplies the structure from which agency can arise.

A smaller interface example:

> "Nothing is sent until you click Submit. Until then, you can edit or delete the draft."

That sentence can do more emotional work than "Stay in control" because the control is observable.

#### Tension inside a real constraint

Tension does not require invented urgency. A constraint creates a real difference between what can and cannot happen. Capacity limits, deadlines, dependency order, compatibility boundaries, sequencing requirements, finite permissions, and irreversible states can all provide narrative tension when they are materially relevant.

**Procedure [B]: State the constraint, the event that activates it, and the available choice. Do not amplify probability, scarcity, or consequence.** Information-gap and threat research support attention to unresolved constraints, but the procedure itself is an applied synthesis rather than a validated universal conversion tactic.

**Boundary:** A constraint that has no bearing on the reader's task is trivia. A constraint whose severity, frequency, or urgency is exaggerated becomes manipulation.

Fictional supplied facts for "Beacon Rooms":

- A workspace supports ten simultaneous editors.
- An eleventh editor cannot enter edit mode.
- Any administrator can remove an editor's active seat.
- After a seat is released, another editor can enter.

Flat:

> "Beacon Rooms supports up to ten concurrent editors."

Tension-led:

> "Ten people can edit at once. An eleventh cannot enter edit mode until a seat is released."

Nothing has been invented. The limit itself carries the tension.

Impermissible:

> "When the eleventh person arrives in the middle of a critical deadline, work grinds to a halt."

"Critical deadline," "arrives," and "work grinds to a halt" are unsupplied events and consequences.

#### A truth-first emotional drafting algorithm

**Rule [A]: Search for emotional structure in evidence before searching for emotional vocabulary.** The defensible mechanisms above depend on concrete representation, real uncertainty, efficacy/control information, narrative sequence, and real constraints.

**Boundary:** A fact set can genuinely contain no emotionally consequential structure. In that case the correct output is flat.

Use this order:

**Fact:** What exactly is known?

**State change:** What begins, stops, becomes unavailable, becomes known, or becomes reversible?

**Human interaction:** What must a real user do, but only if that action is part of the supplied evidence?

**Constraint:** What real limit, dependency, condition, or tradeoff appears?

**Resolution:** What uncertainty can the evidence actually close?

**Agency:** What action can the reader genuinely take, reverse, defer, inspect, or control?

**Language audit:** Remove any sentence that asserts a feeling, motive, frequency, consequence, urgency, prevalence, probability, customer experience, or risk not established by evidence.

The central prohibition is simple: **do not "add emotion." Increase resolution around the emotionally consequential facts.**

### Where emotion is the wrong tool

The premise requires correction. There is direct evidence for some product-context reversals, but there is **not** equally strong comparative evidence showing that emotional framing measurably underperforms plain information for every expert interface, utility microcopy task, or post-purchase message. A truth-constrained authority document should not invent that evidence either.

#### High-deliberation and utilitarian purchases

Geuens, De Pelsmacker, and Faseur experimentally compared emotional and non-emotional advertising across product categories and involvement levels. Their results undermine the blanket view that emotion always improves persuasion. Emotional advertising had clearer advantages for low-involvement and hedonic contexts; those advantages did not generalize cleanly to high-involvement or utilitarian products. Importantly, **congruence** mattered: an emotion that was genuinely relevant to the product could still work, while incongruent emotion was particularly vulnerable in high-involvement contexts.

**Rule [B]: For high-deliberation or strongly utilitarian decisions, do not sacrifice diagnostic information to create an emotional tone.** Direct experimental advertising evidence shows no universal emotional advantage and identifies high involvement and utilitarian contexts as important boundaries.

**Boundary:** "High involvement" does not mean "emotion never works." Product-congruent emotion can still improve evaluation. A founder choosing enterprise backup can care about control, responsibility, or risk while still requiring detailed evidence.

The correct hierarchy is therefore:

> Decision evidence first.  
> Relevant emotion may accompany it.  
> Irrelevant emotion is noise.

#### Expert audiences

There is no A-grade basis in the evidence reviewed here for the blanket law "experts respond better to facts than emotion." Expertise, involvement, domain knowledge, identity, and task type are different variables. High-involvement advertising evidence is not sufficient to convert that slogan into an expert-audience law. The hard-truth conclusion is an evidence gap, not a universal rule.

For expert-facing copy, flatness is justified when the user's immediate task is verification: exact API behavior, compatibility, contractual scope, error semantics, measurement method, security architecture, eligibility, or limitations. That justification is functional, not a claim that experts lack emotion.

Bad:

> "A breakthrough architecture built for teams who demand absolute confidence."

Better when the documented fact is what matters:

> "Requests are encrypted in transit with TLS 1.3. Stored objects use AES-256. Customer-managed encryption keys are not supported."

No emotional upgrade is needed.

#### Utility microcopy

No robust A/B comparative literature in this review establishes a general quantitative penalty for emotional versus plain utility microcopy. Therefore "utility microcopy must be emotionless" should not be presented as an empirical law.

The truth constraint still yields a strong operational result. In validation, permissions, billing, security, destructive actions, and error recovery, emotional copy is wrong whenever it **uses space needed for task information or invents the user's state**.

Error:

> "Oops! Don't worry, we've got you."

The system does not know that worry exists, and the sentence does not identify the problem.

Task copy:

> "Upload failed. Column 6 contains an invalid date. Use YYYY-MM-DD and upload again."

Flat is correct because the job is diagnosis and recovery.

#### Post-purchase communication

There is likewise no defensible blanket finding in this evidence base that emotional post-purchase messages underperform plain messages across all outcomes. Post-purchase contexts range from celebration and onboarding to invoices, failed payments, security notices, shipping exceptions, renewals, cancellations, and incident communication. Treating them as one persuasion category is analytically unsound.

Flatness is correct when the communication's primary value is certainty about **what happened, what was charged, what changed, what the recipient must do, and what happens next**. Emotion may be added only when supported and subordinate to those facts.

A fictional payment receipt needs:

> "Payment received: $480. Invoice NB-1048. Coverage period: September 1-30."

It does not need:

> "Great choice. Your team is going to love what comes next."

The latter invents both evaluation and outcome.

The permission to write flat follows from the core evidence pattern across this reference: emotional interventions are **conditional**, effect sizes are often small to moderate, many effects vary by domain, and even famous findings can fail direct replication. "Accurate, mechanically clear, and flat" is not defective when the reader's problem is uncertainty reduction rather than motivation.

### Folklore appendix

#### "People buy on emotion and justify with logic."

**Grade D as a universal behavioral law.**

The phrase is commonly attributed to Zig Ziglar. The traceable Ziglar sales tradition is closer to "logic makes people think; emotion makes them act" and, importantly, advises salespeople to use **both logic and emotion**. Secondary summaries of *Secrets of Closing the Sale* preserve that formulation. The exact aphorism "People buy on emotion and justify with logic" is widely attributed to Ziglar, but this research did not locate a controlled primary study from him, nor a primary source demonstrating that purchase decisions systematically occur emotionally first and are logically rationalized afterward.

**Earliest traceable origin found:** Zig Ziglar's sales teaching, associated with *Secrets of Closing the Sale*, originally published in the 1980s; later reprints and summaries preserve the logic-emotion prescription. The exact modern sentence has a weaker provenance than the attribution suggests.

**What the source actually establishes:** a practitioner sales doctrine that both logic and emotion matter. It does not measure temporal ordering of emotion and reasoning in purchases, percentage of emotionally caused purchases, or post-hoc rationalization rates.

**Adjudication:** Do not cite this aphorism as behavioral science.

#### "Emotional ads outperform rational ads by 2 to 1."

**Grade D in this universal form.**

The nearest traceable empirical basis is not a randomized experiment showing that an emotional ad generates twice the sales of an otherwise equivalent rational ad. It is the IPA effectiveness-case tradition associated with Les Binet and Peter Field. Popular summaries report figures such as roughly **31 percent versus 16 percent** of cases achieving a defined "very large" business or profit effect for emotional versus rational campaign classifications. That ratio is then converted into "emotion works 2x better."

**Earliest traceable origin found for the numerical retelling:** IPA Databank analyses by Binet, Field, and related authors, later consolidated in *The Long and the Short of It*. The source material concerns submitted advertising-effectiveness cases and categorical incidence of large reported effects, particularly in long-term brand building. It is not an experimental average treatment effect and is not an estimate that emotional creative doubles conversion, ROI, memory, or sales for every campaign.

**What the source actually measured:** proportions of classified campaign cases reporting large business outcomes under particular database definitions and time horizons.

**Adjudication:** The case-database finding is commercially interesting. "Emotional ads outperform rational ads by 2 to 1" is an overgeneralized slogan.

#### "Stories are 22 times more memorable than facts."

**Grade D.**

The claim is repeatedly attributed to cognitive psychologist Jerome Bruner and sometimes to Stanford professor Jennifer Aaker. Stanford material itself repeats that stories can be "up to 22 times" more memorable, but the number does not come with a traceable controlled study establishing that ratio.

A source-tracing investigation reported checking Bruner's *Actual Minds, Possible Worlds*, the work frequently named as the origin, and finding no 22x statistic there. The same audit found the statistic repeated by Aaker without a methodological derivation. Earlier marketing repetition routed the claim through an Insight Demand presentation and then attributed it to Bruner.

**Earliest traceable origin found in the marketing citation chain:** an Insight Demand presentation cited by MarketingProfs, followed by broader attribution to Bruner. No underlying Bruner experiment measuring "22 times" was located.

**What real narrative evidence says instead:** narrative persuasion effects are typically modest. A health-narrative meta-analysis reported an overall effect around **r = .063**, while direct evidence-type meta-analysis has sometimes favored statistical evidence over narrative evidence.

**Adjudication:** Delete the 22x statistic from professional copywriting guidance.

#### "Loss aversion means losses are twice as powerful as gains."

**Grade D as a universal persuasion rule.**

The number has a real mathematical ancestor but the marketing interpretation is wrong. Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 cumulative prospect theory fitted a loss-aversion parameter of approximately **lambda = 2.25** for the particular risky-choice data and model they analyzed. Modern work discussing the literature identifies that 2.25 estimate as the source of the familiar "roughly twice" formulation.

**Earliest traceable quantitative origin:** Tversky and Kahneman, "Advances in Prospect Theory: Cumulative Representation of Uncertainty," 1992. The estimated parameter described the relative slope of a fitted value function in a risky-choice model. It did **not** demonstrate that a loss-framed headline is 2.25 times as persuasive as a gain-framed headline, that losing $100 creates precisely twice the emotion of gaining $100 for every person, or that loss-framed advertising doubles conversion.

The marketing transfer is directly contradicted by message-framing meta-analysis, where gain-versus-loss differences are commonly tiny, null, or context dependent, and some pooled results favor gain framing.

**Adjudication:** Keep "reference-dependent evaluation and loss aversion can occur." Delete "losses are twice as powerful as gains" as a copy rule.

#### "95 percent of purchase decisions are subconscious."

**Grade D.**

The marketing claim is commonly routed to Gerald Zaltman's 2003 *How Customers Think*. The traceable wording associated with the book concerns an estimate that roughly **95 percent of thought, emotion, and learning occurs outside conscious awareness**, not an experiment finding that 95 percent of purchase decisions are made subconsciously.

Source tracing reported that Zaltman's estimate in turn points to George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's *Philosophy in the Flesh*, where the figure is presented as a cognitive-science **rule of thumb** concerning unconscious thought, not a measured share of consumer purchases. The popular retelling changes both the dependent variable and the evidentiary status: "much cognition is nonconscious" becomes "95 percent of purchases are subconscious."

**Earliest traceable marketing origin:** Zaltman's 2003 book popularized the 95 percent figure in a consumer context; the underlying general-cognition estimate predates it and was not a purchase-decision measurement.

**What the source actually said:** approximately 95 percent of broad mental activity such as thought, emotion, and learning may occur without conscious awareness, presented as an estimate rather than a directly measured purchase-decision percentage.

**Adjudication:** "95 percent of purchase decisions are subconscious" is not a valid statistic and should never appear in an evidence-constrained marketing authority document.

#### "Open loops make people remember unfinished information."

**Grade D as a general copywriting rule.**

The traceable scientific ancestor is Bluma Zeigarnik's 1920s work on interrupted tasks. Modern marketing practice transformed an interrupted-task memory hypothesis into a recommendation to leave headlines, emails, videos, and sales sequences unresolved. A 2025 meta-analysis found **no general memory advantage for unfinished tasks**. The more defensible surviving effect is the tendency to resume interrupted tasks, associated with Ovsiankina, and even that is conditioned by situation and involvement.

**Earliest traceable origin:** Zeigarnik's 1927 interrupted-task studies for the memory claim; Ovsiankina's 1928 work for task resumption.

**What the modern evidence measured:** recall of interrupted versus completed laboratory tasks and resumption of interrupted tasks, not click-through rates, email opens, sales-page completion, or customer trust.

**Adjudication:** Use real information gaps because curiosity has evidence. Do not cite the Zeigarnik effect as proof that unresolved copy is inherently more memorable.
