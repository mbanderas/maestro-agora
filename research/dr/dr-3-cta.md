# CTAs and Action Microcopy

## Evidence-graded craft reference

Assumption: "CTA" here means a selectable marketing or acquisition control that advances the reader toward an offer, such as a button or action link. Product-interface commands are covered separately because a command such as "Delete workspace" or "Save and continue" has a different job from a marketing CTA. Grades follow the supplied scale exactly: **A** for multiple controlled studies, meta-analysis, or large replicated field data; **B** for one controlled study or one large documented field test with method; **C** for named practitioner evidence with documented results but insufficient method or replication for B; **D** for a widely repeated rule without a traceable evidentiary basis strong enough for A, B, or C.

### What a CTA is doing, and what outcome naming should mean

A CTA can perform four separable jobs. They should not be collapsed into "use an action verb."

**Predict the consequence of activation [Grade C].** The control must give enough information scent for the reader to predict where it leads or what it will do. This is the most defensible load-bearing requirement for label copy. Nielsen Norman Group's link-label research treats specific labels as promises about the destination, and W3C's link-purpose guidance requires users to be able to determine link purpose from the label or its programmatically associated context. This evidence is usability and accessibility evidence, not proof of a universal conversion-rate lift. [NN/g, "Better Link Labels"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/better-link-labels/), [NN/g, "A Link Is a Promise"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/link-promise/), [W3C, Link Purpose](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-link-only.html).  
**Boundary:** The label does not have to carry all meaning by itself when adjacent, programmatically associated context makes the purpose unmistakable. A button below a heading "Choose a payment method" can use "Continue" more safely than an isolated homepage button can. W3C explicitly allows context to supply some link purpose at Level A.

**Name the immediate user payoff, deliverable, or state change [Grade C].** Direct CTA testing supports making the value or concrete consequence more explicit, but the evidence is almost entirely practitioner A/B testing with incomplete sample reporting. Michael Aagaard reported a 14.79% lift when "Order Information and Prices" became "Get Information and Prices", then a 38.26% lift from the same conceptual change on a Danish sister site. That is useful directional evidence and a same-operator replication, not an independent replication. [Unbounce, Aagaard case studies](https://unbounce.com/conversion-rate-optimization/small-changes-increasing-conversion-rates/).  
**Boundary:** "Outcome" must mean what this control or the immediately initiated flow can truthfully deliver. A demo button labeled "Reduce close time" names a hoped-for downstream business result, not the consequence of clicking. It is less accurate than "See how exceptions are reconciled" if the control opens that demonstration.

**Set the apparent commitment level [Grade C].** Labels communicate how consequential the next step feels. Practitioner tests show that this can override the simplistic "specific outcome always wins" rule. MarketingExperiments reported that "Continue" produced 81% more clickthrough than "Become a Member" in one test, because the latter made the commitment sound immediate and larger than the actual next step. Another multi-treatment test reported "Get Started Now" at 11% above "Start Free Trial", while "Try Now" ran 26% below "Start Free Trial". Sample sizes were not published in the articles, so these remain Grade C. [MarketingExperiments, CTA commitment test](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/3-keys-successful-cta-from-childhood), [MarketingExperiments, five-way CTA test](https://marketingexperiments.com/copywriting/cta-button-copy-reduce-clickthrough).  
**Boundary:** A low-commitment label becomes misleading when the next step is actually consequential. "Continue" is poor copy if clicking places an order, starts billing, sends an application, or deletes data.

**Reduce perceived risk [Grade A for the existence of a possible reassurance backfire; Grade C for CTA-specific implementation].** Risk reduction usually belongs in adjacent microcopy rather than inside the main CTA. Controlled research on privacy messages shows that reassurance can reduce behavior when it makes a previously background risk salient. Zeng and colleagues used a field experiment plus a controlled experiment and found that security-focused privacy assurance reduced purchase probability and purchase amount relative to no such assurance, mediated by increased privacy concern. [Journal of Business Ethics DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-020-04626-x).  
**Boundary:** This does not mean "never reassure." Where risk is already salient, legally material, technically unusual, or demonstrably blocking completion, factual reassurance can answer an existing objection instead of creating one.

The practical hierarchy is therefore:

1. **Consequence clarity is load-bearing [Grade C].** The reader must be able to predict what activation means.
2. **Commitment calibration is load-bearing when the action carries cost, identity disclosure, payment, scheduling, or other consequential commitment [Grade C].**
3. **Risk reduction is load-bearing only where a real concern exists [Grade A/C].** Unprompted reassurance can reverse direction.
4. **Outcome framing is a useful craft heuristic, not a universal experimentally established law [Grade C].** Practitioner tests support it in some contexts and contradict it in others.

**Replacement production rule [Grade C]: Name the immediate user-visible consequence, concrete deliverable, or honestly initiated task at the commitment level the control actually triggers. Do not name only interface motion. Do not promise a downstream result the control cannot itself deliver.** The evidence supports specificity and expectation-setting more strongly than a strict grammatical distinction between "outcomes" and "actions." [NN/g, "Better Link Labels"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/better-link-labels/), [GOV.UK button guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/), [Unbounce, Aagaard CTA research](https://unbounce.com/conversion-rate-optimization/small-changes-increasing-conversion-rates/).  
**Boundary:** Literal action labels remain correct when the action itself is the user's task or when literalness is needed for safety: "Pay", "Save and continue", "Download report", "Send application", and "Delete workspace" are not failures merely because they describe actions. GOV.UK explicitly differentiates "Continue" from "Save and continue" according to what the service actually does.

A useful classification follows.

| Classification | What the label names | Fictional example | Default judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Immediate outcome | A state or answer the interaction directly produces | "Get my close-readiness score" | Strong when the score actually follows |
| Artifact | A concrete object the reader receives or opens | "Download the exception report" | Usually valid; the artifact can itself be the outcome |
| Consequential action | The real user task or state change | "Start trial", "Pay invoice", "Send application" | Valid when commitment is accurate |
| Interface motion | Navigation or interface behavior without enough destination value | "Explore", "See more", "Go" | Weak unless context supplies the missing purpose |
| Slogan | An aspiration with no inspectable click consequence | "Move with confidence" | Reject as a primary action label on clarity grounds, not on proven conversion superiority of another word class |

This classification refutes the strong version of the current production rule. **Artifact framing is not intrinsically inferior to outcome framing [Grade C].** W3C specifically notes that the name of a document or application can be sufficient to communicate a link's purpose, while GOV.UK recommends action labels that accurately name the operation performed. [W3C, Link Purpose](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/link-purpose-in-context.html), [GOV.UK button guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/).  
**Boundary:** A bare artifact noun such as "Ledger" or "Report" is different from "Download reconciliation report". The first identifies an object; the second identifies a usable consequence.

For the two production examples:

- `"Explore the platform"` is action-framed and low-information. The verb describes navigation but leaves the reader to infer what they will learn or accomplish. **Reject as an isolated primary CTA [Grade C].** A fictional replacement might be `"Compare exception workflows"` if the destination actually provides that comparison. [NN/g homepage guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/).  
  **Boundary:** `"Explore workflow templates"` can be correct when the destination is intentionally an explorable catalog and "workflow templates" is the user's goal.

- `"See the reconciliation flow"` is not automatically noncompliant. It identifies an artifact or informational destination, but it still leaves the informational payoff underspecified. **Require a stronger completion when the page can name what the reader will resolve [Grade C].** `"See how unmatched entries are resolved"` is stronger if that is exactly what appears next. [NN/g, "A Link Is a Promise"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/link-promise/).  
  **Boundary:** If the literal purpose is watching a walkthrough, `"Watch the reconciliation walkthrough"` is more accurate than inventing a business outcome.

The practitioner test record does not support a stronger universal rule:

| Tested wording contrast | Reported effect | Evidence judgment |
|---|---:|---|
| "Order Information and Prices" to "Get Information and Prices" | +14.79%; related Danish sister-site change +38.26% | **C.** Direction repeated within the same operator/site family; sample sizes and independent replication absent. [Source](https://unbounce.com/conversion-rate-optimization/small-changes-increasing-conversion-rates/) |
| "Request a Quote" to "Request Pricing" | +95% clickthrough | **C.** Strong single-site reported effect; sample size absent. [Source](https://marketingexperiments.com/copywriting/cta-optimization-button-copy-test-increases-click-rate-95) |
| "Become a Member" to "Continue" | "Continue" +81% clickthrough | **C.** Counterexample to "more outcome-specific always wins"; sample size absent. [Source](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/3-keys-successful-cta-from-childhood) |
| "Start Free Trial" versus "Get Started Now" | "Get Started Now" +11% | **C.** Generic-seeming lower-commitment copy won in this test. [Source](https://marketingexperiments.com/copywriting/cta-button-copy-reduce-clickthrough) |
| "Start Free Trial" versus "Try Now" | "Try Now" -26% | **C.** Shows that merely reducing words or commitment language does not guarantee improvement. [Source](https://marketingexperiments.com/copywriting/cta-button-copy-reduce-clickthrough) |
| "Start your free trial" versus "Start my free trial" | approximately +90% reported for first person | **C.** Famous single-site result with incomplete sample reporting; no independent published replication found. [Source](https://marketingexperiments.com/email-marketing/email-optimization-a-single-word-change-results-in-a-90-lift-in-sign-ups) |

**First-person framing rule [Grade C]: Treat "my" versus "your" as a testable ownership-framing variable, not a house rule.** Aagaard reported the approximately 90% first-person lift above and later stated that first-person variants had produced lifts across several tests, but the additional experiments were not published with enough method to establish replication. [Unbounce, Aagaard on first-person CTA tests](https://unbounce.com/a-b-testing/failed-ab-test-results/).  
**Boundary:** The evidence does not establish that `"Start my trial"` beats `"Start trial"`. The famous test compared first person with second person, not first person with a pronounless control. Compact controls, localized interfaces, established command conventions, and literal system actions can all favor the pronounless version.

**Do not encode "Start my trial > Start your trial > Start trial" as an evidence ranking [Grade D].** No robust published three-arm evidence establishing that ordering was located. The production authority can permit first-person framing when it makes ownership natural, but it cannot claim universal conversion superiority.

### Commitment laddering

The psychological literature does not justify a generic funnel doctrine in which any small click makes a later large commitment more likely.

**Do not use a low-commitment CTA because "a small yes causes a bigger yes" [Grade A].** The foot-in-the-door effect is real enough to have generated a large literature, but it is weak and conditional. Beaman and colleagues' meta-analysis covered 120 experimental groups and concluded that the effect was replicable but weak, with a large share of studies producing null or contrary results. Burger's later review identified multiple mechanisms and substantial dependence on procedure and context. [Beaman et al., meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167283092002), [Burger, review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1207/s15327957pspr0304_2).  
**Boundary:** Foot-in-the-door effects can occur when the first request is voluntary, meaningful, identity-relevant, and sufficiently related to the later request. That does not make a generic content click, demo view, or newsletter signup a reliable causal primer for purchase.

The replication record strengthens that restriction. A later direct replication program revisiting Freedman and Fraser's classic procedure across Ukraine and Poland in 2003 and 2013 obtained a significant effect in one earlier sample, no effect in another, and insignificant effects in the later replications. The authors concluded that the classic effect had not reproduced reliably. [Psychological Reports, direct replication](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033294119872208).

**Treat micro-conversions as routing and measurement events, not as a presumed compliance engine [Grade A for rejecting the causal assumption; Grade C for marketing implementation].** A "See benchmark" click can reveal interest and route a low-readiness reader to evidence, but its value must be measured by downstream behavior. The foot-in-the-door literature does not license assuming that the click itself creates purchase readiness. Practitioner CRO literature uses "micro yes" language, but that mechanism is not established for ordinary web funnels. [Beaman meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167283092002), [Unbounce practitioner discussion](https://unbounce.com/conversion-rate-optimization/small-changes-increasing-conversion-rates/).  
**Boundary:** A micro-conversion can still be commercially valuable as lead capture, segmentation, qualification, or delivery of evidence. The prohibition is against claiming an automatic psychological escalation effect.

**Match the CTA's commitment to the evidence state of the reader [Grade C].** "Evidence state" means what the reader can reasonably know at that decision point: what the offer is, whether it fits the problem, approximate or exact cost where relevant, mechanism, proof, terms, material constraints, and material risk. The CTA should ask for the largest commitment the page and prior journey have earned, not mechanically the smallest possible one. Practitioner tests in which "Continue" beat "Become a Member" and "Get Started Now" beat one trial formulation demonstrate that perceived commitment is part of the wording effect. [MarketingExperiments commitment test](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/3-keys-successful-cta-from-childhood), [five-treatment test](https://marketingexperiments.com/copywriting/cta-button-copy-reduce-clickthrough).  
**Boundary:** The page itself is not the reader's entire evidence state. A returning customer, branded-search visitor, referral, or procurement-approved buyer can arrive ready to purchase even when the landing page is brief.

A workable ladder is therefore not "always ask small first." It is:

| Evidence state | Appropriate primary ask | Fictional label |
|---|---|---|
| Problem recognized, solution not yet understood | Obtain explanatory evidence | "Compare reconciliation methods" |
| Solution understood, fit uncertain | Evaluate fit | "Check Atlas against my workflow" |
| Fit established, commercial details missing | Resolve commercial condition | "See team pricing" |
| Fit, terms, and risk resolved | Begin commitment | "Start trial" |
| Buyer has chosen product and terms | Complete transaction | "Buy Core plan" or "Pay $240" |

**Do not interpose a low-commitment CTA in front of a ready buyer without a necessary reason [Grade C].** Checkout and form research consistently finds that unnecessary fields and steps add interaction cost; Baymard's large checkout research reports that roughly 17% of surveyed abandonments are attributed to checkout complexity, while its usability work emphasizes total form burden more than nominal step count. This is not a randomized test of "See demo" versus "Buy", so the CTA-specific rule remains Grade C. [Baymard, checkout form complexity](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields).  
**Boundary:** Eligibility checks, legal disclosures, security verification, configuration choices, inventory checks, and other genuinely necessary steps are not wasted friction.

A ready fictional buyer who has selected ArcLedger Pro, reviewed pricing, compared plans, and returned from the checkout should not be forced through `"Explore ArcLedger"` because it looks less intimidating. The low-commitment CTA is now a detour. A secondary `"Review plan details"` link can remain available without replacing `"Continue to payment"` as the primary action. **Primary action should serve the highest evidenced intent; secondary action can catch unresolved intent [Grade C].**  
**Boundary:** Mixed-audience homepages are different from campaign landing pages. They may need multiple routes because visitors legitimately arrive in different evidence states.

### Friction, cost, and reassurance

The reassurance line is not decorative trust copy. Its useful functions are narrower: remove uncertainty about the next event, answer a known objection, disclose a requirement before the click, state reversibility, or make a material time cost legible.

**State what happens next when the button label cannot carry the whole sequence [Grade C].** GOV.UK's service patterns distinguish controls such as "Continue", "Save and continue", "Pay", and task-specific submission labels because the consequence matters. Nielsen Norman Group likewise recommends specific, substantial button and link labels rather than generic navigation language. [GOV.UK button component](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/), [NN/g button guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/button-states-communicate-interaction/).  
**Boundary:** Do not duplicate what is already obvious. A sub-label saying "Click to continue to the next step" under a button labeled "Continue to shipping" adds words without resolving uncertainty.

Fictional pattern:

`Check my close readiness`  
`You'll answer 5 questions, then see your score immediately.`

The second line earns space because it clarifies sequence and time. It should not say `"Fast and easy"` because that supplies no inspectable information.

**State a missing requirement only when absence of that requirement is a live decision factor [Grade C].** `"No credit card required"` is useful when trial signups in the category commonly imply a card requirement. `"No sales call"` is useful where a "Get pricing" flow could plausibly route to sales. The evidence supports removing known form anxiety and explaining seemingly unnecessary requests, not indiscriminately adding reassurance phrases. Baymard repeatedly observes hesitation when checkout fields appear unrelated to the transaction, particularly unexplained phone-number requirements. [Baymard, unnecessary information](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-experience-seemingly-unnecessary-information), [Baymard, phone-field rationale](https://baymard.com/blog/explain-phone-number-field).  
**Boundary:** Absence copy can create a concern. Do not put `"No hidden malware"` beneath a harmless calculator signup merely because malware is technically absent.

**Explain why sensitive or apparently unnecessary data is required, close to the field [Grade C].** Baymard's checkout usability research finds that users can perceive unexplained phone-number requests as privacy invasion or misuse risk and recommends a concise explanation, or making the field optional where it is not genuinely required. [Baymard, phone-number field research](https://baymard.com/blog/explain-phone-number-field).  
**Boundary:** Explanation does not legitimize unnecessary collection. Remove an unnecessary field instead of writing persuasive copy for it.

**Do not introduce a latent risk category merely to reassure against it [Grade A].** Zeng and colleagues' field and controlled experiments found that security-oriented privacy assurance could reduce purchasing by increasing privacy concern. In practitioner testing, Aagaard also reported a 24% reduction in downloads from a privacy reassurance variation that introduced "spam" language near an ebook form. The latter lacks published sample size and is Grade C on its own, but it points in the same direction as the controlled privacy research. [Journal of Business Ethics study](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-020-04626-x), [Aagaard practitioner test](https://unbounce.com/conversion-rate-optimization/small-changes-increasing-conversion-rates/).  
**Boundary:** When the risk is already salient, a precise assurance can be necessary. A medical-record upload, financial authorization, identity check, or unusual permission request should not pretend the concern does not exist.

The craft distinction is:

- Bad reassurance for fictional NovaLedger newsletter signup: `"We hate spam too. Your email is completely safe."`
- Better, when relevant: `"One monthly product note. Unsubscribe in one click."`
- Better for a sensitive fictional file-analysis form: `"Files are deleted after the analysis completes."`

The second and third lines specify behavior instead of invoking broad claims of trustworthiness. **Prefer verifiable process facts over generic trust adjectives [Grade C].** GOV.UK service guidance similarly favors concrete explanations of what information is used for, such as explaining that an email address is used to send a receipt. [GOV.UK email-address pattern](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/email-addresses/).  
**Boundary:** A process fact that is not material to the user's decision is still clutter. Proximity does not make irrelevant reassurance useful.

**State reversibility when reversibility is a material reason the action is safer than it appears [Grade C].** For reversible product choices, `"Cancel before activation"` or `"You can change this later"` can resolve a genuine commitment concern. In destructive flows, however, do the reverse: make irreversibility explicit and prefer an undo mechanism when feasible. Nielsen Norman Group recommends undo over routine confirmation where the action can safely be reversed and warns that excessive confirmations produce habituation. [NN/g, confirmation dialogs](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-dialog/).  
**Boundary:** Never imply reversibility when operational recovery requires support intervention, an exception, or data restoration the product cannot guarantee.

**State time cost when it is known, stable, and material [Grade C].** Time information is expectation-setting copy, not a magic conversion phrase. Form-complexity research supports the underlying proposition that user effort matters, but there is no strong CTA-specific literature establishing that `"Takes 2 minutes"` has a general positive effect. [Baymard, checkout complexity](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields).  
**Boundary:** Do not publish optimistic time estimates from internal happy-path testing. `"About 2 minutes"` becomes deceptive microcopy if a normal user must locate account numbers, wait for verification, or complete ten conditional fields.

A reassurance line therefore passes only when it answers a concrete question:

`What happens next?`  
`What will this require from me?`  
`Can I reverse it?`  
`How long will the meaningful next step take?`  
`What will happen to sensitive information I am about to provide?`

**Do not fill unused reassurance space with generalized claims such as "secure", "risk-free", "easy", or "no hassle" [Grade A for the privacy-salience risk; Grade C for the broader writing rule].** These claims either lack operational content or can activate the very concern they are intended to suppress.  
**Boundary:** Regulated disclosures, independently verified security claims, guarantees, and concrete contractual rights can be material evidence. They should be stated precisely rather than converted into generic reassurance language.

### CTA count, competition, repetition, and label consistency

Choice overload is frequently used to justify "one CTA per page." The psychology literature does not support that universal inference.

**Do not infer that more choices automatically reduce action [Grade A].** Scheibehenne, Greifeneder, and Todd's meta-analysis found an average choice-overload effect close to zero across the literature, with substantial heterogeneity. Chernev, Bockenholt, and Goodman's later meta-analysis identified systematic moderators, including choice-set complexity, task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and whether the chooser has a clear decision goal. [Journal of Consumer Research meta-analysis](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/37/3/409/1827647), [Journal of Consumer Psychology meta-analysis](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002).  
**Boundary:** Overload becomes more plausible when options are hard to compare, preferences are weak or unformed, the task is difficult, and the user lacks a clear goal. More choice can be neutral or beneficial when alternatives are familiar, meaningfully differentiated, and easy to screen.

**Minimize simultaneously competing, equally weighted actions when the reader must resolve a difficult or uncertain decision [Grade A for the choice-complexity premise; Grade C for the CTA implementation].** The purpose is not to hit a numeric CTA count. The purpose is to avoid making the reader solve an avoidable choice problem before taking the intended next step. [Chernev et al. meta-analysis](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002), [CXL practitioner discussion](https://cxl.com/blog/call-to-action-examples/).  
**Boundary:** A comparison page, marketplace, dashboard, or mixed-intent homepage can legitimately present several actions because choosing among them is the task.

**Use primary versus secondary hierarchy to encode intended priority when two valid next steps serve different evidence states [Grade C].** A fictional ArcLedger pricing page can use `"Start Core plan"` as the primary action and `"Compare plan limits"` as a visually subordinate secondary action. The second route serves unresolved fit without asking the ready buyer to take it. This is consistent with practitioner CRO practice and general visual-hierarchy guidance, but no broad randomized CTA study establishes a fixed conversion effect. [CXL CTA review](https://cxl.com/blog/call-to-action-examples/).  
**Boundary:** A secondary action at nearly identical visual weight is not meaningfully secondary. Conversely, hiding a genuinely necessary alternative can make the interface less usable.

**Distinguish CTA goals from CTA instances [Grade D as a conversion rule].** Repeating the same `"Start Core plan"` action after the hero, comparison section, and FAQ does not create three conceptual offers. It creates three access points to one offer. Practitioner sources commonly recommend repeating a single conversion goal on sufficiently long pages, but published controlled evidence establishing a general lift or optimal repetition interval was not located. [Unbounce landing-page copy guide](https://unbounce.com/landing-page-copywriting/).

There is therefore no defensible rule such as "repeat the CTA every two screens" or "never repeat a CTA." **Repeated same-goal CTAs are permitted, not empirically mandated [Grade D].** Their defensible purpose is interaction convenience after new evidence sections, not a claimed universal conversion effect.

**Use one canonical label for one materially identical action across a surface [Grade C].** W3C states as best practice that links to the same destination use the same descriptions and connects this to consistent identification of components with the same functionality. Nielsen Norman Group's consistency heuristic likewise argues that users should not have to wonder whether different words, situations, or actions mean the same thing. [W3C, Link Purpose](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-link-only.html), [NN/g, Consistency and Standards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/consistency-and-standards/).  
**Boundary:** Context can legitimately change the local wording when the same destination serves a different understandable purpose. `"Pricing"` in global navigation and `"Compare plans"` in a hero can point to the same page without necessarily creating confusion, because one is a destination label and the other is a task invitation. The canonical rule is strongest when controls claim to perform the same action.

For a fictional product, these labels should trigger review:

- Nav: `"Try Atlas"`
- Hero: `"Get started"`
- Pricing table: `"Create account"`
- Footer: `"Start free"`
- Modal: `"Begin"`

If all five initiate the identical account-creation flow under identical terms, the reader must repeatedly determine whether the words denote the same commitment. **Normalize the action unless a documented contextual reason requires the variant [Grade C].** The evidence supports consistency and recognition generally; no controlled study located quantifies the conversion penalty from nav/hero/pricing/footer label divergence specifically. [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/), [W3C consistent link-purpose guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-link-only.html).  
**Boundary:** Do not force literal string identity across different component roles. A navigation noun and a transactional button can legitimately use different syntax while preserving the same underlying concept.

The frequently implied "repeated decision cost" is therefore an **inference from recognition and consistency research, not a measured CTA conversion effect [Grade C].** Different labels require semantic remapping; the direct size of that cost on conversion remains unmeasured in the evidence located.

### Urgency and scarcity

Scarcity has stronger academic evidence than most CTA-copy folklore, but the result is heterogeneous and does not justify invented urgency.

**Real scarcity can increase purchase likelihood [Grade A].** Ladeira and colleagues' 2023 meta-analysis retained 37 articles and 335 effect sizes from experimental studies. The random-effects mean effect of scarcity on purchase decisions was **Cohen's d = 0.2776, 95% CI 0.2243 to 0.3209**, with **I2 = 68%**, indicating substantial heterogeneity. Excess-demand scarcity was materially stronger than restricted-supply scarcity in that corpus. Limited-quantity and limited-time cues did not significantly differ from each other as moderators. [Ladeira et al., full article](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21816).  
**Boundary:** The average is not a CTA-label effect and not a guarantee. Product type, source of scarcity, social signaling, seasonality, involvement, and other contextual variables change the result.

A separate 2022 meta-analysis by Barton, Zlatevska, and Oppewal synthesized **416 effect sizes from 131 studies** and likewise concluded that scarcity effects depend strongly on the source of scarcity and product context. Demand-based scarcity was most effective for utilitarian products, supply-based scarcity for experiences, and time-based scarcity for high-involvement products. [Journal of Retailing article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022435922000434).

**Use scarcity copy only to report a real constraint, not to manufacture motivation [Grade A for the behavioral premise; Grade C for the operational rule].** A legitimate fictional message is `"Enrollment closes Friday at 17:00 because onboarding begins Monday"` when enrollment really closes then. `"Act now before it's too late"` is not justified merely because scarcity generally has a positive average effect. [Ladeira et al.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21816), [Barton et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022435922000434).  
**Boundary:** A real stock constraint can still be misleading if the wording exaggerates its practical consequence, such as implying permanent unavailability when replenishment is routine and imminent.

**Manufactured urgency carries a trust cost as well as a regulatory cost [Grade B for controlled user evidence; legal constraint separately applies by jurisdiction].** Controlled research on deceptive countdown timers found that such interfaces can produce skepticism and aversion toward the offer or site rather than merely increasing action. The CHI 2023 work used an online experiment with 245 participants and examined deceptive timers as a dark-pattern mechanism. [ACM CHI study](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3544549.3585735).  
**Boundary:** The study concerns deceptive countdown implementations. It does not establish that truthful deadlines produce the same trust penalty.

Current enforcement makes the boundary concrete. On **May 22, 2026**, the UK High Court confirmed a settlement after Emma Sleep admitted consumer-law breaches involving misleading countdown timers, false high-demand messages, and misleading discount claims. The CMA announced the court-endorsed result on **May 28, 2026**. [UK CMA, Emma Sleep case](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/court-endorses-cma-action-as-emma-sleep-agrees-to-change-sales-practices). The European Commission's Digital Fairness review likewise identifies fake countdown timers that create urgency as a problematic manipulative practice. [European Commission, Digital Fairness Fitness Check Q&A](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/fi/qanda_24_4909). The FTC has separately identified manipulative dark-pattern techniques as a consumer-protection enforcement concern. [FTC dark-pattern announcement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/09/ftc-report-shows-rise-sophisticated-dark-patterns-designed-trick-trap-consumers).

**Production boundary for urgency [Grade C as an operational synthesis]: An urgency or scarcity statement is publishable only when all of the following are true: the underlying constraint is real now; the scope of the constraint is stated accurately; waiting can actually change availability, price, timing, or another material condition; the message disappears or changes when the constraint changes; and the organization can identify the operational fact that substantiates the claim.** Official enforcement and the experimental literature both support treating truthful scarcity and manufactured pressure as different classes. [UK CMA case](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/court-endorses-cma-action-as-emma-sleep-agrees-to-change-sales-practices), [European Commission](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/fi/qanda_24_4909), [scarcity meta-analysis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21816).  
**Boundary:** There is no exception for a fabricated timer that happens to increase conversion. Effectiveness does not make a false constraint true.

**Do not encode a numeric "scarcity wears out after N exposures" rule [Grade D].** The two modern scarcity meta-analyses establish heterogeneous effects but do not provide a transportable exposure-count or time-to-decay function for repeated CTA exposure. No defensible general half-life was located.

The correct treatment of repeated urgency is therefore binary before it is tactical: if a deadline resets, "only 3 left" is not tied to actual stock, "high demand" is fabricated, or substantially identical terms continue after a claimed final deadline, the copy fails the truth test. The authority should remove it rather than optimize it. **Truth validation precedes conversion optimization [Grade C].**  
**Boundary:** Continuously changing real inventory, dynamic capacity, auctions, event seats, and genuine enrollment windows can legitimately produce repeated urgency messages when the displayed state remains accurate.

### Anti-patterns, non-button action copy, and the drafting test

**"Learn more" [Grade C].** As a standalone label, it usually withholds the topic or destination. Nielsen Norman Group specifically criticizes generic "Learn More" links because repeated generic links provide poor information scent and are hard to differentiate when users scan. [NN/g, "Learn More Links: You Can Do Better"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/learn-more-links/).  
**Correct condition:** It can be acceptable when the surrounding component unambiguously supplies the object and programmatic context, such as a card headed `"Flux reconciliation rules"` followed by a single `"Learn more"` link. A more self-contained `"Learn about reconciliation rules"` remains preferable where links are scanned out of context.

**"Get started" [Grade C, not a blanket anti-pattern].** It is vague about destination and commitment when isolated, but there is direct practitioner evidence that it can win. MarketingExperiments reported `"Get Started Now"` 11% above `"Start Free Trial"` in one multi-treatment test. Sample size was not published, so the result is C. [MarketingExperiments test](https://marketingexperiments.com/copywriting/cta-button-copy-reduce-clickthrough).  
**Correct condition:** Use it for a genuine multi-step onboarding or setup process when "starting" accurately describes the commitment and the adjacent copy makes the process clear. Do not use it as a euphemism for payment, sales contact, or a commitment the label obscures.

**"Submit" [Grade C for preferring a more specific consequential label; Grade D for "never use Submit"].** Dan Zarrella analyzed more than 40,000 HubSpot customer landing pages and found pages whose button retained the default `"Submit"` label had lower average conversion than pages using other wording. He also ran a context-free button-choice exercise with more than 400 people. This was observational and context-free evidence, not a randomized 40,000-page experiment, so it does not establish causal universal inferiority. [HubSpot, Zarrella analysis](https://blog.hubspot.com/blog/tabid/6307/bid/6737/don-t-submit-to-landing-page-button-text.aspx).  
**Correct condition:** `"Submit"` is defensible in technical, administrative, or expert interfaces where submitting a job, query, build, or record is literally the user's conceptual task and the object is already obvious. For a consumer form, `"Send application"`, `"Create account"`, or `"Get report"` usually carries more consequence information.

**"Explore" [Grade C].** Nielsen Norman Group explicitly includes generic `"Explore"` among labels that can fail to tell users what they will get. [NN/g homepage design principles](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/).  
**Correct condition:** `"Explore templates"` or `"Explore the map"` can be correct when browsing is the intended task and the object is named. The failing form is bare or underspecified motion, not the verb itself.

**"Discover" [Grade C by application of descriptive-link evidence; no word-specific controlled test located].** The problem is the same as with `"Explore"`: an abstract discovery verb can hide destination and consequence. Specific labels improve information scent. [NN/g, Better Link Labels](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/better-link-labels/).  
**Correct condition:** `"Discover available integrations"` is defensible on a browsing surface where the reader does not know which options exist. Bare `"Discover"` is not.

**"Click here" [Grade C for accessibility and usability, not conversion].** W3C identifies `"click here"` and `"more"` as nondescript link text under its link-purpose guidance. Descriptive text helps users determine whether to follow a link and is especially important when assistive technology exposes links outside their visual context. [W3C failure example](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/failures/F84), [W3C descriptive link technique](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/html/H30).  
**Correct condition:** It can appear as incidental instructional prose in a tutorial teaching mouse interaction, where clicking itself is the instructional object. It should not be the sole label of an ordinary navigation or conversion link.

**"Request information" [Grade C].** It does communicate that the response is a request rather than instant delivery, but `"information"` leaves the object unspecified. A practitioner test changing `"Request a Quote"` to the more expectation-matched `"Request Pricing"` reported a 95% clickthrough lift, showing that the noun can materially change response, though the test lacks published sample size. [MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/copywriting/cta-optimization-button-copy-test-increases-click-rate-95).  
**Correct condition:** Use `"Request information"` when a manual, open-ended information request is literally the service and no narrower artifact can truthfully be named. Where the request is specifically for pricing, specifications, eligibility, or a consultation, name that object instead.

**Bare product names [Grade C, not a blanket anti-pattern].** W3C notes that a document or application name can itself be sufficient to communicate link purpose when the purpose is to open that document or application. [W3C Link Purpose](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/link-purpose-in-context.html).  
**Correct condition:** `"Atlas"` can be correct in a product switcher, tab set, application launcher, or navigation menu. It is weaker as a transactional button when the user needs to know whether clicking buys Atlas, opens Atlas, compares Atlas, or starts an Atlas trial.

**"Continue" [Grade C, not a blanket anti-pattern].** GOV.UK explicitly recommends `"Continue"` when a service advances without saving and `"Save and continue"` when it saves. This is literal consequence copy. [GOV.UK button guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/).  
**Correct condition:** Linear flows in which the next step is already clear and the click does not conceal a new commitment. It becomes wrong when the click pays, publishes, sends, deletes, signs, or otherwise performs a consequential operation.

**"Next" [Grade C by the same interaction principle].** It is adequate for low-consequence wizard navigation when the sequence itself supplies meaning, but inadequate when it conceals a state-changing operation. [GOV.UK button guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/).  
**Correct condition:** Moving between explanatory or editable wizard steps. Use the real action on the final step.

**"OK" and "Yes" on consequential dialogs [Grade C].** Confirmation-dialog guidance favors labels that identify the actual operation, especially for destructive or irreversible actions, because generic assent requires the user to reparse the prompt before knowing what the button will do. [NN/g confirmation dialogs](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-dialog/), [Apple alerts guidance](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/alerts).  
**Correct condition:** `"OK"` is acceptable for dismissing a purely informational message where acknowledgement is the sole harmless action. `"Yes"` can be acceptable in a simple, unmistakable binary question with no hidden consequence, though action-specific labels remain safer for high-impact operations.

The slogan-shaped class requires a different adjudication.

**There is no credible controlled evidence that slogan-shaped CTA labels as a class underperform action labels as a class [Grade D].** The literature and practitioner evidence support descriptive information scent, accurate consequence, and commitment matching. They do not establish an experimentally validated lexical category called "slogan-shaped labels" with a known conversion penalty. CXL explicitly notes that CTA performance depends heavily on page context and gives examples where unconventional language can be suitable. [CXL CTA analysis](https://cxl.com/blog/call-to-action-examples/).

The editorial rejection can still stand, but its basis should be **operational ambiguity**, not a fabricated conversion theorem:

| Label | Grade | Diagnosis | Narrow condition where it can be correct |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Take control" | D | Names an aspiration, not the next consequence | Campaign headline; or a literal control-center entry point where adjacent copy makes that meaning unmistakable |
| "Move with confidence" | D | Emotional payoff with no inspectable destination | Brand line or supporting copy, not an isolated transactional control |
| "Fix what matters" | D | Does not identify what will be fixed or what click initiates | A remediation interface after the specific prioritized issue list is already visible |
| "See the difference" | D | "Difference" has no referent in the label | A tightly contextualized before/after comparison where the two states are immediately visible in adjacent copy |
| "Unlock your potential" | D | Remote aspirational outcome, not a controllable consequence | Campaign slogan; not justified as a primary product action by current evidence |
| "Transform your results" | D | Promises a downstream result that one click cannot deliver | Campaign proposition, not an action label |
| "Start your journey" | D | Indicates beginning but conceals the actual process | A named, genuinely journey-like onboarding program when adjacent copy identifies the program and commitment |
| "Make it count" | D | No destination, artifact, state change, or commitment information | Campaign line or decorative supporting copy |
| "Get clarity" | D as a bare label | Names a mental outcome without its object | Can become valid when the click directly produces a defined diagnostic, for example `"Get clarity on close risks"` |

The enforceable editorial rule is therefore: **Reject slogan-shaped primary CTAs when the label cannot answer what the reader will have, know, start, change, send, open, or commit to next [Grade C for the underlying clarity requirement; Grade D for any claim that slogans universally convert worse].** [NN/g, Better Link Labels](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/better-link-labels/), [W3C, Link Purpose](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-link-only.html).  
**Boundary:** A slogan can remain nearby as persuasion copy. The restriction concerns the action label's informational job.

**Non-button action copy follows a stricter literal-consequence standard than marketing CTA copy [Grade C].** The user is already operating a system. The copy should support execution, recovery, consent, and safety rather than restate marketing value. GOV.UK, Apple, Android, Material, and NN/g guidance converge strongly on explicit actions, actionable errors, in-context permissions, system-status communication, and safer destructive flows.  
**Boundary:** Product interfaces can still explain value where users need motivation or context, particularly onboarding and empty states. Value copy should not replace the literal action label on consequential controls.

**Forms [Grade C]: Ask for what is needed, explain non-obvious requirements, and make the final button name the actual consequence.** `"Create account"` is preferable to `"Unlock your future"` on an account form. Error messages should identify what went wrong and how to correct it. [GOV.UK error-message component](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message/), [GOV.UK validation guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/validation/).  
**Boundary:** Highly familiar low-risk forms can use conventional terse labels when context makes the consequence unmistakable.

**Confirmations [Grade C]: Confirm the completed state, supply any reference or receipt the user needs, and state the next meaningful step.** GOV.UK uses a dedicated confirmation-page pattern and recommends a check-answers stage before many transactions. [GOV.UK confirmation pages](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/confirmation-pages/), [GOV.UK check answers](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/check-answers/).  
**Boundary:** Trivial reversible actions do not need elaborate confirmation screens.

**Errors [Grade C]: Say what is wrong and how to fix it; preserve valid user input.** GOV.UK explicitly requires both diagnosis and correction guidance, while NN/g recommends explicit, human-readable, precise, constructive error messages positioned close to the problem. [GOV.UK error messages](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message/), [NN/g form-error guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/errors-forms-design-guidelines/).  
**Boundary:** Do not over-explain when a compact field-specific correction gives the complete remedy.

**Permission prompts [Grade C]: Ask in context, state why the requested access is needed for the action the user just chose, and handle denial without coercive copy where the product can still function.** Android explicitly recommends associating permissions with specific user actions, explaining less-obvious sensitive permissions, and degrading gracefully after denial. Apple requires purpose strings explaining why protected-resource access is needed. [Android runtime-permission guidance](https://developer.android.com/training/permissions/requesting), [Apple privacy guidance](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy).  
**Boundary:** Do not add a separate pre-permission lecture when the system prompt and immediate context already make the purpose obvious. Platform rules also constrain what custom priming is permitted.

**Empty states [Grade C]: Explain the system state and provide a direct path into the relevant task.** NN/g identifies system-status communication, learnability, and direct task pathways as the principal jobs of useful empty states. [NN/g, empty states](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/).  
**Boundary:** An intentionally empty completion state, such as `"No unresolved exceptions"`, may need no growth-oriented CTA at all.

**Destructive-action confirmations [Grade C]: Name the destructive operation explicitly, provide an obvious safe exit, and prefer undo over repetitive confirmation when safe recovery is feasible.** NN/g recommends confirmations for severe or irreversible actions and warns against habituation from overuse. [NN/g confirmation dialogs](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-dialog/).  
**Boundary:** Extra confirmation is appropriate for rare, severe, irreversible operations. It is counterproductive for routine reversible edits.

The operational drafting test is a gate, not a style prompt.

1. **Run the first-person completion test [Grade C].** Write: `"When I activate this, I will ____."` Fill the blank without using the physical mechanics `"click"`, `"tap"`, or an equally empty motion verb. The answer must identify something the reader will know, receive, start, send, change, open, compare, reserve, buy, or otherwise accomplish. If the answer is only `"see more"` or `"explore"`, ask what concrete information or object becomes available. [NN/g, Better Link Labels](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/better-link-labels/).  
   **Boundary:** `"Watch video"`, `"Open map"`, and `"Download report"` pass because the action itself is the user's concrete task.

2. **Classify the candidate as Outcome, Artifact, Consequential Action, Interface Motion, or Slogan [Grade C].** Outcome, artifact, and consequential-action labels can pass. Interface-motion labels require enough object or contextual information to predict the destination. Slogans fail as isolated primary controls unless the adjacent context makes the consequence unmistakable. [W3C Link Purpose](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-link-only.html), [NN/g homepage labels](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/).  
   **Boundary:** Classification is diagnostic, not a hierarchy. Artifact and consequential-action labels are not automatically inferior to outcome labels.

3. **Run the truth-distance test [Grade C].** Ask whether the control itself or the immediately initiated flow can deliver what the label says. Reject remote benefit claims that depend on later user behavior or uncertain business results. `"Get reconciliation report"` passes if the flow produces the report. `"Cut close time by 40%"` fails as a button label unless the control somehow guarantees that outcome. [NN/g, "A Link Is a Promise"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/link-promise/).  
   **Boundary:** A page headline can state substantiated product outcomes more broadly. The action label has the narrower job of describing the next commitment.

4. **Run the commitment match [Grade C].** Write down the evidence already available to this reader: problem definition, mechanism, fit, proof, price, terms, risk, and any information supplied by prior journey or customer status. Compare it with the commitment requested by the CTA. Lower the ask only when material uncertainty remains; raise it when the reader is demonstrably ready. [MarketingExperiments commitment test](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/3-keys-successful-cta-from-childhood), [Baymard checkout complexity](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields).  
   **Boundary:** Never infer that a smaller CTA is psychologically superior merely because it is smaller. Foot-in-the-door effects are too weak and unstable for that production rule. [Beaman et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167283092002).

5. **Run the friction and reassurance test [Grade A/C].** List the specific reasons a rational reader could hesitate at this action. Add microcopy only for a real, material concern that the main copy does not already answer. Prefer exact next-step, requirement, reversibility, handling, or time information. Delete reassurance that introduces a new hazard category. [Journal of Business Ethics privacy study](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-020-04626-x), [Baymard phone-field research](https://baymard.com/blog/explain-phone-number-field).  
   **Boundary:** Sensitive, regulated, or unusually consequential actions often require explicit risk and privacy information even when mentioning the topic increases salience.

6. **Run the competition and surface-consistency audit [Grade A/C].** Count distinct decisions, not button instances. Identify which one is primary for the evidence state this page serves. Then inventory every label for that same action in the nav, hero, body, pricing table, modal, and footer. Canonicalize materially identical actions; document each intentional variant. [Choice-overload moderator meta-analysis](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002), [W3C consistent identification guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-link-only.html).  
   **Boundary:** Multiple routes are legitimate when the page intentionally serves multiple goals or evidence states. Nav nouns and transactional button phrases need semantic consistency, not necessarily character-for-character identity.

7. **Run the urgency proof test [Grade C].** For every deadline, stock count, `"high demand"` statement, or price-expiry claim, identify the real operational fact, current source of truth, expiry behavior, and what materially changes if the reader waits. Remove the message if any link in that chain is missing. [UK CMA, 2026 Emma Sleep case](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/court-endorses-cma-action-as-emma-sleep-agrees-to-change-sales-practices), [European Commission Digital Fairness Q&A](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/fi/qanda_24_4909).  
   **Boundary:** Genuine live inventory, real event capacity, and actual enrollment deadlines pass when the interface remains synchronized with reality.

8. **Run the system-action override for non-marketing UI [Grade C].** When the control sends data, changes settings, grants permission, commits payment, publishes content, or destroys data, literal system consequence outranks benefit-oriented marketing language. Use `"Send application"`, `"Allow camera for receipt scanning"`, or `"Delete workspace"` rather than euphemisms. [GOV.UK button guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/), [Android permission guidance](https://developer.android.com/training/permissions/requesting), [NN/g confirmation guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-dialog/).  
   **Boundary:** Supporting copy can explain the benefit. The consequential control itself should remain literal.

[NN/g 2010 original research](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention-original-research/), [NN/g 2018 follow-up](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/) | First-viewport content receives disproportionate attention. That does not imply a universal CTA-placement rule. MarketingExperiments documented a case with a **304%** higher conversion rate for a below-fold treatment and explicitly warned that multiple changes meant the lift could not all be attributed to CTA position. [MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/email-marketing/email-optimization-a-single-word-change-results-in-a-90-lift-in-sign-ups) |
| **"Outcome copy always beats action copy."** | **D** as a universal rule; **C** as a specificity heuristic | Practitioner CTA doctrine is traceable through Michael Aagaard's early 2010s case-study work, including `"Order Information and Prices"` to `"Get Information and Prices"`. [Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-rate-optimization/small-changes-increasing-conversion-rates/) | The test record contains counterexamples. `"Continue"` beat `"Become a Member"` by a reported 81%, and `"Get Started Now"` beat `"Start Free Trial"` by a reported 11% in separate practitioner tests. Artifact and literal-action labels are also correct when they accurately name the immediate task. The defensible rule is immediate consequence clarity, not grammatical outcome supremacy. [MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/3-keys-successful-cta-from-childhood), [five-treatment test](https://marketingexperiments.com/copywriting/cta-button-copy-reduce-clickthrough) |
| **"Every CTA must start with an action verb."** | **D** as a conversion rule | No traceable controlled origin establishing universal conversion superiority was located. UX systems such as GOV.UK do often use action-oriented commands, but as a clarity convention rather than a demonstrated universal marketing effect. [GOV.UK button guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/) | A verb is useful when it clarifies a task, but the production failure is not lack of a verb. `"Explore"` contains a verb and can still be useless. A navigation label such as `"Pricing"` can contain no verb and still communicate the destination perfectly. |
| **"Slogan-shaped labels always underperform action labels."** | **D** | No controlled study treating slogan-shaped labels as an experimental class was located. The rejection appears to derive from broader clarity, information-scent, and conversion-copy practice rather than direct class-level testing. The editorial standard is stronger when it states the observable defect instead of inventing an effect size. |
| **"Repeat the CTA every N screens or sections."** | **D** | Practitioner landing-page guidance commonly recommends repeating the same conversion action on long pages, but no general controlled origin establishing an optimal interval was located. [Unbounce landing-page copy guide](https://unbounce.com/landing-page-copywriting/) | Repetition can reduce navigation cost after new evidence, but there is no defensible universal cadence. Count distinct competing decisions separately from repeated instances of the same decision. |
