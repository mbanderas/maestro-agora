# Awareness, Sophistication, and Message-Market Match

## Prompt 4: Evidence-graded craft reference

**Assumptions used throughout:** "awareness" means the knowledge and readiness state of a particular reader, not the brand-awareness metric used in media measurement. "Sophistication" means accumulated familiarity with competing category claims and persuasion conventions, not intelligence, education, or technical competence. "Mechanism" means an explanation of how the product produces the promised result. "Coined term" means a proprietary or newly invented label for that mechanism or category.

**Grading convention:** The supplied A-D scale has no separate grade for evidence-informed synthesis. Exact copy-routing prescriptions have not been experimentally tested as 25 awareness-by-sophistication cells. They are therefore graded **D | PI**, meaning practitioner inference, even where adjacent components have A or B evidence. This prevents indirect academic evidence from being presented as direct validation.

### Awareness staging

Eugene M. Schwartz introduced the practitioner lineage in *Breakthrough Advertising* in 1966. The book's second chapter is explicitly organized around "your prospect's state of awareness" and moves from the "most aware" prospect through progressively less informed conditions to the "completely unaware" market. The now-standard labels "unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, most aware" are a normalized practitioner vocabulary for those conditions rather than an experimentally derived psychological taxonomy. The adjacent third chapter addresses market sophistication separately. ([chapter outline](https://spdrdng.com/posts/summary-of-breakthrough-advertising-by-eugene-m-schwartz))

| Schwartz lineage | What the state claims about the reader | Copy implication in the practitioner model | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | The reader does not recognize the relevant problem, desire, or category in the form the seller uses. | Start from something already experienced or believed. Do not require category vocabulary as the price of entry. | C as practitioner lineage; D as experimentally validated prescription |
| Problem aware | The reader recognizes the undesirable condition or desired result but does not yet have a settled solution model. | Name or intensify the problem and establish that a solution class exists. | C lineage; D causal prescription |
| Solution aware | The reader understands that solutions of this general kind exist but has not selected this product. | Establish category fit and meaningful differentiation. | C lineage; D causal prescription |
| Product aware | The reader knows the product but has unresolved questions about fit, credibility, superiority, risk, or price. | Resolve objections and make comparative proof easy to inspect. | C lineage; D causal prescription |
| Most aware | The reader knows the product and its core promise and is near action. | Lead with the offer, terms, availability, risk reduction, or final decision criterion rather than re-educating the category. | C lineage; D causal prescription |

**[Grade C] Rule: Treat Schwartz's awareness model as a durable practitioner segmentation heuristic, not as a measured law of consumer cognition.** The primary lineage is a working copywriter's 1966 book and decades of practitioner reuse, not controlled validation of those five exact states. ([practitioner source](https://spdrdng.com/posts/summary-of-breakthrough-advertising-by-eugene-m-schwartz)) **Boundary:** The framework stops being useful when the five labels conceal heterogeneous motives, prior experiences, decision roles, or occasions inside one nominal stage.

The academic literature supports the weaker proposition behind Schwartz: prior knowledge and decision phase change what information people process. Bettman and Park found that consumers with moderate knowledge processed more available information than low- or high-knowledge consumers, that more knowledgeable consumers tended to process by brand, and that evaluation patterns changed across phases of choice. ([Journal of Consumer Research, 1980](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/7/3/234/1820883)) Brucks subsequently examined product-class knowledge as a determinant of pre-purchase information search using a search-task method. ([Journal of Consumer Research, 1985](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/12/1/1/1791043))

**[Grade B] Rule: Change information structure when prior category knowledge changes.** A novice and a knowledgeable evaluator should not be assumed to process the same evidence in the same way. ([Bettman and Park](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/7/3/234/1820883); [Brucks](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/12/1/1/1791043)) **Boundary:** Knowledge does not produce a monotonic "more expert means more information" relationship. Bettman and Park found the middle-knowledge group did more processing than either extreme, so a simple novice-to-expert staircase is wrong.

Wood and Lynch sharpen the boundary. Across three studies, higher-prior-knowledge consumers learned less about a new product than lower-knowledge consumers, not because they lacked capacity but because they paid less attention during encoding. ([Journal of Consumer Research, 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/29/3/416/1800953))

**[Grade A] Rule: Do not equate expertise with willingness to absorb introductory explanation.** Knowledgeable readers can become less attentive to information that appears redundant with what they already know. ([Wood and Lynch, three studies](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/29/3/416/1800953)) **Boundary:** The demonstrated effect concerns new-product learning under conditions in which prior knowledge can produce complacency. It does not mean experts always read less, nor that technical evidence should be removed when it is decision-relevant.

Academic stage models predate and run alongside Schwartz without validating him. Lavidge and Steiner's 1961 hierarchy proposed awareness, knowledge, liking, preference, conviction, and purchase, but the original paper explicitly recognized that an impulse purchase could occur without passing through the preceding states. ([Journal of Marketing, 1961](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1248516)) Modern customer-journey research likewise treats the process as multi-touch and channel-spanning rather than a single mechanically linear funnel. Lemon and Verhoef's review centers consideration, search, purchase, post-purchase experience, and interactions across multiple touchpoints. ([Journal of Marketing, 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jm.15.0420))

**[Grade B] Rule: Use awareness stages as message states, not as a mandatory sequence every person must traverse.** Controlled work establishes phase- and knowledge-dependent processing, while classic funnel theory itself contains bypass cases. ([Bettman and Park](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/7/3/234/1820883); [Lavidge and Steiner](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1248516)) **Boundary:** For a deliberately sequenced educational program, the marketer can impose a sequence of exposures. That is a campaign architecture, not evidence that the buyer's internal process naturally follows the same order.

The evidential verdict is therefore precise: **Schwartz's exact five-stage framework has practitioner status, not experimental status.** The literature supports stage-sensitive and knowledge-sensitive processing. It does not establish the unique validity of five stages, their labels, their completeness, or a universal linear progression.

### Market sophistication

Schwartz's separate "market sophistication" construct describes what happens to advertising strategy as a market accumulates similar claims. The practitioner sequence is conventionally summarized as: first state the claim directly; then enlarge or intensify it; when amplified claims become exhausted or unbelievable, feature a new mechanism; when mechanisms themselves proliferate, enlarge or differentiate the mechanism; finally, move toward identification when claims and mechanisms have lost novelty. Contemporary summaries track that sequence back to the third chapter of *Breakthrough Advertising*. ([Schwartz book notes](https://taylorpearson.me/bookreview/breakthrough-advertising/); [rights-holder practitioner treatment](https://breakthroughadvertisingbook.com/mastering-market-sophistication-the-key-to-winning-in-any-industry/))

| Sophistication state | Schwartz-lineage prescription | Evidential verdict |
|---|---|---|
| S1: fresh market | State the desired result directly. | C for practitioner lineage; D for universal causal rule |
| S2: repeated direct claims | Enlarge, intensify, specify, or otherwise outbid the familiar claim. | C lineage; D causal rule |
| S3: claim saturation | Shift attention to the mechanism that makes the result possible. | C lineage; D causal rule |
| S4: mechanism saturation | Differentiate, enlarge, refine, or dramatize the mechanism itself. | C lineage; D causal rule |
| S5: claim/mechanism exhaustion | Shift toward identification, affinity, experience, or another dimension outside the exhausted claim contest. | C lineage; D causal rule |

**[Grade C] Rule: Use Schwartz's sophistication stages as a competitive-message audit, not as a scientifically established market-development law.** Named practitioner lineage is clear, but no controlled evidence in this review validates the exact five-stage progression or proves that each prescribed move is necessary at each stage. ([Schwartz notes](https://taylorpearson.me/bookreview/breakthrough-advertising/)) **Boundary:** Different segments inside the same nominal market can have radically different histories of exposure, so a category cannot safely be assigned one sophistication number for every audience.

The strongest academic anchor is the Persuasion Knowledge Model. Friestad and Wright proposed that people accumulate knowledge about persuasion agents' goals and tactics and use that knowledge to interpret and cope with selling attempts. ([Journal of Consumer Research, 1994](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/21/1/1/1853712)) Campbell and Kirmani then tested this logic experimentally. Across several experiments, consumers used persuasion knowledge when an ulterior persuasion motive was accessible; cognitive capacity changed the effect when the motive was less accessible. ([Journal of Consumer Research, 2000](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/27/1/69/1791556))

**[Grade A] Rule: Assume that experienced audiences can interpret the selling tactic itself, not merely the literal claim.** Repeated market exposure gives consumers material from which to infer persuasive intent, and experimentally increasing accessibility of that intent changes evaluations of the influence agent. ([Campbell and Kirmani, several experiments](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/27/1/69/1791556)) **Boundary:** Persuasion knowledge is not automatically activated. Campbell and Kirmani show that motive accessibility and available cognitive capacity determine whether it is used.

Advertising repetition supplies a second, narrower anchor. Schmidt and Eisend's meta-analysis of advertising repetition examines the exposure levels at which consumer responses peak rather than supporting an unlimited "more repetition is better" model. ([Journal of Advertising, 2015](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00913367.2015.1018460)) This is academically adjacent to Schwartz's intuition that familiar messages can lose marginal persuasive power, but it does not prove that the remedy is a unique mechanism.

**[Grade A] Rule: Do not assume that repeating or intensifying the same advertising claim produces monotonically increasing persuasion.** Meta-analytic evidence supports exposure-response limits. ([Schmidt and Eisend](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00913367.2015.1018460)) **Boundary:** Repetition can still increase memory and familiarity, and the point of diminishing or adverse return varies by outcome, execution, interval, and context. This evidence does not establish a universal "claim wearout stage."

Claim skepticism supplies a third anchor. Experimental consumer research has found systematic differences in skepticism toward different types of advertising claims, including greater skepticism toward less objectively verifiable claims. The implication is not that "claims stop working" but that claim form and verifiability affect how much discounting occurs.

**[Grade B] Rule: In a claim-saturated market, increase verifiability before increasing rhetorical intensity.** Claim skepticism research supports greater resistance to claims that are harder to verify. ([primary bibliographic record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v16y1990i4p433-41.html)) **Boundary:** Skepticism varies by attribute and claim form; this does not mean quantified, objective, or technical claims are automatically believed.

The mechanism prescription therefore receives a narrower verdict than copywriting lore gives it. **A mechanism can differentiate and restore explanatory specificity in a sophisticated comparison context. No reviewed experiment establishes Schwartz's stronger rule that mechanism naming is the necessary third stage of market evolution.** Persuasion knowledge supports the diagnosis of increased tactic recognition; advertising wearout supports diminishing returns from repeated exposure; claim-skepticism research supports stronger demands for credibility. The move from those findings to "name a unique mechanism" remains practitioner inference.

### The routing consequence

The crucial distinction is that **awareness and sophistication are separate axes**. Awareness belongs to the reader. Sophistication describes the competitive message environment the reader has actually encountered. A mature category can contain a first-time reader. A technically expert buyer can be unaware that the seller's newly framed problem has a category name. A long-time category buyer can be product-unaware with respect to a new entrant.

The production rule under review is:

> "Buyer sophistication decides whether naming a mechanism helps: a buyer comparing three vendors wants it named, a reader meeting the category for the first time will not pay the cost of learning a proper noun."

**Verdict: qualify. [Grade D | PI]** The first half conflates sophistication with awareness and evaluation task. A buyer comparing vendors usually has high solution awareness, often high category knowledge, and a task that rewards discriminating attributes. Those conditions make a meaningful mechanism label useful. The second half has stronger adjacent evidence: unfamiliar jargon imposes processing cost, including when definitions are supplied. In an N=650 experiment, jargon reduced processing fluency and engagement with scientific information even when definitions accompanied the terms. ([Shulman et al., 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X20902177)) The exact mechanism-naming rule itself has not been directly tested.

**[Grade B] Rule: Do not make an unfamiliar coined term the prerequisite for understanding an unfamiliar problem.** Experimental jargon research shows that introducing specialized language can reduce processing fluency even when definitions are provided. ([Shulman et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X20902177)) **Boundary:** A term stops functioning as costly jargon when the target audience already knows it, when its meaning is transparent from ordinary language, or when a category label itself supplies a useful organizing structure.

**[Grade D | PI] Corrected routing rule:** *Awareness decides how much conceptual context the opening must supply. Sophistication decides how much differentiation and persuasion resistance the message must overcome. Name a mechanism early only when the reader already understands enough of the problem and solution space for the mechanism name to reduce comparison cost rather than create vocabulary cost.*

Three evidence-backed constraints bound that table.

**[Grade A] Rule: Experienced or skeptical audiences require credibility, not merely greater claim intensity.** Persuasion-knowledge experiments establish that consumers use knowledge of persuasive motives when those motives become accessible. ([Campbell and Kirmani](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/27/1/69/1791556)) **Boundary:** Low motive accessibility and restricted cognitive capacity reduce use of persuasion knowledge.

**[Grade B] Rule: Plain-language meaning should precede an unfamiliar proper noun when the reader lacks the relevant vocabulary.** Jargon experimentally reduces processing fluency. ([Shulman et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X20902177)) **Boundary:** Familiar technical terminology used by a technical evaluator is not functioning as unfamiliar jargon.

**[Grade A] Rule: For genuinely unfamiliar products, familiar conceptual structures can aid understanding.** Moreau, Markman, and Lehmann ran two experiments showing that the first plausible familiar category label given for a really new product significantly affected categorization, expectations, and preferences. ([Journal of Consumer Research, 2001](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/27/4/489/1810433)) **Boundary:** Category framing can mislead as well as clarify. In the same research, consumers struggled to transfer information appropriately from multiple categories unless the advertiser constrained what should be transferred.

The practical hinge follows: **precision differentiates only after the reader has enough structure to interpret what the precision differentiates.** Before that point, a coined mechanism is not precision from the reader's perspective. It is an extra object to learn. The exact sentence is **D | PI**; its strongest adjacent support is the B-grade jargon evidence and the A-grade categorization evidence above.

### Diagnosing stage and the cost of mismatch

Stage should be inferred from behavioral evidence, not assigned from the product alone. A "technical" product does not imply a sophisticated reader. A mature category does not imply an aware visitor.

Sponsored-search field research provides useful but non-causal support for query-based diagnosis. Jansen and Schuster analyzed nearly seven million records spanning 33 months and more than $56 million in sponsored-search activity and classified search phrases into funnel-oriented states such as awareness, research, decision, and purchase; behavior differed across phrase classes. ([Penn State research record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/bidding-on-the-buying-funnel-for-sponsored-search-and-keyword-adv/))

**[Grade C] Rule: Treat the search query as the strongest routinely available pre-click awareness signal when search is the traffic source.** A query like "why invoices don't match contracts" supplies a different state signal from "revenue assurance software," "CloseSignal alternatives," or "CloseSignal pricing." Large observational search data support meaningful behavioral differences between intent classes. ([Jansen and Schuster](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/bidding-on-the-buying-funnel-for-sponsored-search-and-keyword-adv/)) **Boundary:** Query taxonomies are imposed by researchers and marketers; one phrase can encode multiple motives, and the cited field data come from a particular sponsored-search environment rather than a universal experiment.

| Available signal | What it legitimately tells the writer | Typical inference | Rule and grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Search query | The explicit language and immediate research task that produced the visit. | Symptom query: earlier. Category query: solution aware. Vendor comparison: solution/product aware. Brand + price/demo: product/most aware. | **[C]** Use as the strongest common pre-click state signal, not a deterministic label. |
| Traffic source | The context in which the person encountered the message. | Broad social/display tends earlier; retargeting, branded search, partner referral, and customer email tend later. | **[D \| PI]** Use directionally only. |
| Referring content | What argument, vocabulary, and problem frame the reader has already consumed. | A technical comparison article can pre-educate the visitor; a general-interest story usually cannot. | **[D \| PI]** Continue from demonstrated context instead of restarting the argument. |
| Channel | The attention contract and probable intent. | Search is task-driven; interruptive social requires more context; documentation and marketplaces imply stronger category intent. | **[D \| PI]** Adapt commitment and assumed context to channel. |
| Page depth and prior site behavior | What the person has already done on the property. | Pricing, comparison, implementation, security, or repeat product-page visits can indicate later evaluation. | **[D \| PI]** Use for subsequent messages and analysis, not to infer what an anonymous first-view visitor knew before landing. |
| Brief/customer research | Direct statements about triggers, alternatives considered, vocabulary, objections, buying task, and prior solution experience. | Can override generic channel assumptions. | **[D \| PI]** Prefer direct audience evidence over generic funnel stereotypes. |

A critical distinction belongs in production systems: **page depth is not a valid justification for an advanced opening on the page that generated that depth.** It is downstream evidence. Using it to rationalize the original treatment reverses cause and effect. **[Grade D | PI]**

When stage is unknown, the safe fallback is not "write for unaware" and not "write for sophisticated." It is a staged surface.

**[Grade D | PI] Fallback rule:** *Open with a recognizable problem or desired outcome in ordinary language; identify the familiar category when one exists; gesture at the mechanism in plain English; introduce the coined mechanism only after its usefulness is understood; put high-detail proof below or behind clear evaluative signposts; make the first CTA low to medium commitment unless the channel itself demonstrates purchase intent.*

This fallback has direct adjacent support from jargon and overload research but has not itself been experimentally tested as a landing-page treatment. Jargon can impair fluency even with definitions. ([Shulman et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X20902177)) Choice-overload evidence shows that excess information or options do not create a simple universal penalty, but overload becomes more likely under identifiable conditions of choice-set complexity, task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and effort-minimizing goals. Chernev, Bockenholt, and Goodman reached that conclusion in a meta-analysis of 99 observations with N=7,202. ([Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2015](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002))

**[Grade A] Rule: Do not use "information overload" as a blanket argument for less information. Reduce complexity where the reader lacks a decision structure, and retain detail when the task requires it.** Choice-overload meta-analysis identifies strong moderators rather than a universal main effect. ([Chernev et al.](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002)) A separate meta-analysis of 50 experiments and 63 conditions found an average choice-overload effect near zero with substantial variation across conditions, further rejecting a universal "more choice always hurts" rule. ([Scheibehenne, Greifeneder, and Todd, 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v37y2010i3p409-425.html)) **Boundary:** Large or complex choice sets do create overload under the moderators identified by Chernev and colleagues; qualified evaluators can instead benefit from additional discriminating information.

The two directions of stage error have different costs:

| Wrong guess | Immediate message failure | Evidence status | Commercial symptom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Message is too advanced for the reader | Undefined concepts, jargon, comparison criteria, and mechanism labels arrive before the reader has a schema for them. | Jargon cost B; exact stage-mismatch rule D \| PI. | Confusion, shallow engagement, abandonment, or a conclusion that the product is "not for me." |
| Message is too basic for the reader | The page repeats things the reader already knows and delays the discriminating information required for evaluation. | Expert inattention A; exact landing-page rule D \| PI. | Skimming, perceived sameness, return to comparison search, or inability to shortlist. |
| Message is too promotional for a persuasion-aware reader | The buyer recognizes the tactic and infers selling motive instead of processing only the surface claim. | A. | Discounted claims, lower sincerity judgments, demand for independent verification. |
| CTA is too advanced for stage | The page asks for sales commitment before enough belief or organizational readiness exists. | C for B2B lead-timing field observation; exact CTA rule D \| PI. | Low-quality "leads," no-shows, premature handoffs, qualification waste. |
| CTA is too weak for a ready buyer | The page makes a qualified buyer consume education instead of completing the intended transaction. | D \| PI. | Added steps, sales friction, lost high-intent sessions. |

"Bounces" require special treatment. **[Grade D | PI] Rule: Never report bounce as evidence that awareness mismatch caused failure without an experiment or diagnostic evidence.** Bounce is an observable behavior with many possible causes, including technical performance, source mismatch, accidental clicks, weak relevance, price discovery, and completed one-page information tasks. The defensible claim is that stage mismatch can create processing and relevance costs, not that it has a fixed bounce-rate effect.

"Premature detail" requires the same discipline. **[Grade B] Rule: Detail is premature when understanding it requires specialized vocabulary the intended reader has not yet acquired.** Jargon research directly demonstrates processing-fluency cost. ([Shulman et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X20902177)) **Boundary:** Technical detail is not premature for an evaluator whose task is to inspect that detail. Removing it can create the opposite mismatch.

For B2B qualification, recent field data show why CTA staging matters operationally. In 6sense's 2024 buyer study, early-stage form fillers were often converted into marketing-qualified leads and delivered to sales before they were ready, leading the authors to argue that many "lead quality" problems are timing problems. The study covered 2,509 recent B2B buyers. ([6sense 2024 Buyer Experience Report](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/2024-buyer-experience-report/))

**[Grade C] Rule: Do not treat an early educational conversion as proof of sales readiness.** Large B2B survey evidence documents the lead-timing problem. ([6sense](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/2024-buyer-experience-report/)) **Boundary:** A high-value consultative sale can legitimately involve salespeople early as educators. "Early" does not mean "sales should stay away"; it means the commitment should match the buyer's task.

### Category creation and category entry

When the buyer has no stable comparison vocabulary, the first job is not necessarily to invent a new category. It is to give the new thing an intelligible reference structure.

Moreau, Markman, and Lehmann studied "really new products," where buyers must construct new knowledge rather than simply modify an existing product representation. In two experiments, the first plausible category label supplied significantly influenced categorization, expectations, and preferences. Consumers could draw from multiple categories, but transfer became more effective when the advertiser constrained what information should be carried over from each category. ([Journal of Consumer Research, 2001](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/27/4/489/1810433))

**[Grade A] Rule: Give an unfamiliar product a familiar reference category before asking the reader to process a wholly new category vocabulary.** Controlled experiments show that familiar category labels materially shape how really new products are understood. ([Moreau, Markman, and Lehmann, two experiments](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/27/4/489/1810433)) **Boundary:** The first plausible category can anchor expectations incorrectly. Category framing should specify which attributes transfer and which do not.

Analogy has stronger experimental support than the category-creation doctrine. Roehm and Sternthal conducted four experiments on new-product appeals using analogies. Analogies were persuasive when recipients could map the relationship from the familiar base product to the unfamiliar target and devoted enough cognitive resources to perform that mapping. Expertise with the base, processing training, and positive mood could improve comprehension and persuasion. ([Journal of Consumer Research, 2001](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/28/2/257/1899200))

**[Grade A] Rule: For a novel product, use analogy only when the shared relationship is easy to map and the reference object is genuinely familiar to the intended reader.** Four experiments support those conditions. ([Roehm and Sternthal](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/28/2/257/1899200)) **Boundary:** Analogy loses persuasive power when the reader lacks mapping ability or cognitive resources. A clever analogy that requires its own explanation reverses the benefit.

A reliable category-entry construction is therefore:

> "It is like [known category] for [known task], except [critical difference]."

**[Grade D | PI] Rule:** Use that structure to borrow an existing slot before introducing a new label. The structure is synthesis, not a directly tested copy formula; its components are supported by the A-grade categorization and analogy findings above.

Explicit "category creation" as a corporate strategy has much weaker evidence. The modern category-design doctrine was codified prominently in *Play Bigger* in 2016, which argues that a company should design and build a category while building the company and seek to dominate that category. ([Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Play_Bigger.html?id=xC89CgAAQBAJ)) This is practitioner strategy and retrospective case interpretation, not randomized or quasi-experimental evidence that inventing a named category causes market leadership.

**[Grade D] Rule rejected: "You must create a new category to win."** The universal requirement has no controlled primary support in the reviewed literature. Category labels demonstrably affect consumer understanding, but that cognitive finding does not imply that every company should declare a new market category.

**[Grade D | PI] Replacement rule:** *Create new category vocabulary only when existing categories predict the product incorrectly in a way that materially obstructs evaluation. Otherwise position against the nearest useful reference category and spend the reader's learning budget on the product difference.* This is practitioner synthesis. The A-grade boundary from Moreau et al. matters: category labels exert strong expectations, so a needlessly new category forfeits useful prior knowledge, while a misleading old category transfers the wrong expectations.

This is also where mechanism naming and category naming must not be confused. A mechanism answers "how does this work?" A category answers "what kind of thing is this?" **[Grade D | PI] Rule:** Resolve the category question before requiring a novel mechanism proper noun when both are unfamiliar. Making the reader learn a novel noun for the thing and a second novel noun for its causal process in the same opening compounds vocabulary cost.

### B2B committee dynamics

Organizational buying research rejects the fiction of a single homogeneous "B2B buyer." Webster and Wind's foundational 1972 organizational-buying model treated the purchase as a process embedded in organizational, interpersonal, individual, and environmental factors rather than a unitary consumer decision. ([Journal of Marketing](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1250972)) Johnston and Bonoma later examined buying-center composition and interaction patterns for capital-equipment and industrial-service purchases in 31 firms, finding meaningful variation in group structure and interaction by purchase type and organizational conditions. ([Journal of Marketing, 1981](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224298104500312))

**[Grade C] Rule: Model a complex B2B purchase as a buying group with different information tasks, not as one persona with one stage.** The buying-center concept has a long practitioner and empirical organizational-buying lineage, including field examination across 31 firms. ([Johnston and Bonoma](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224298104500312); [Webster and Wind](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1250972)) **Boundary:** Low-value, routine, owner-operated, or single-authority purchases can collapse the buying group into one or two people.

Recent B2B field data make the multiplicity concrete. 6sense's 2025 research drew on nearly 4,000 buyer responses across North America, Asia-Pacific, and Europe, the Middle East, and Africa; its sample included technology, services, manufacturing, and other purchases, with a reported median purchase value around $200,000 to $300,000 and a $25,000 study minimum. The report describes typical buying groups of more than ten people and an average evaluation set of about five vendors. ([6sense 2025 Buyer Experience Report](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/))

**[Grade C] Rule: For complex B2B pages, assume simultaneous reading by people with different criteria unless account research proves otherwise.** Large current survey data show buying groups commonly involve many participants. ([6sense 2025](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/)) **Boundary:** The numerical group-size finding is specific to the study's recent B2B sample, which is concentrated in technology, services, and manufacturing and excludes purchases below its threshold. It is not a universal "ten-person rule."

One page therefore has two jobs that should not be collapsed into one sentence. The economic buyer needs the business consequence, risk, cost, strategic fit, and confidence that adoption will work. The technical evaluator needs architecture, mechanism, integration boundaries, security, performance, implementation, and failure modes.

**[Grade D | PI] Rule: Serve mixed-stage committees with layered copy, not averaged copy.** The first layer should establish the shared problem, outcome, category, and consequential differentiator in language every relevant role can parse. The next layers should expose role-specific proof with explicit labels such as "Security," "Implementation," "Architecture," "ROI," and "Procurement." This is a design inference from buying-group research, not a controlled copy test.

A useful committee surface therefore looks like this:

| Layer | Economic buyer sees | Technical evaluator sees | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hero | Business problem, result, category, decisive distinction. | Enough specificity to know the claim is technically meaningful. | D \| PI |
| Immediate proof | Outcome magnitude, credible customers or evaluation result, time/risk implication. | Evidence that the claim is not pure positioning language. | D \| PI |
| Mechanism section | Why the approach produces the business result. | Exact process, boundaries, dependencies, and trade-offs. | D \| PI |
| Role modules | ROI, adoption, change cost, commercial terms. | Integration, security, architecture, controls, performance. | D \| PI |
| CTA layer | Business case, evaluation, commercial conversation. | Technical brief, sandbox, audit, architecture review. | D \| PI |

**[Grade D | PI] Rule: Do not make the hero carry every committee member's proof burden.** A hero that includes enough security, architecture, economics, procurement, and mechanism terminology to satisfy every evaluator becomes an information-design failure. Let the shared layer establish relevance and route specialist readers to deeper evidence.

**[Grade D | PI] Rule: Do not solve the committee problem by deleting technical specificity.** That merely reverses the mismatch. Give the economic reader an interpretable top layer and the evaluator immediate access to precision.

The mechanism naming rule changes in committees: **[Grade D | PI] name the mechanism in or near the opening when it is already a recognized comparison criterion for the technical evaluator, but pair the name with a plain-language consequence so that the economic buyer does not need the proper noun to understand the value.** For example: "CloseSignal traces each invoice back to the governing contract term through its Contract-to-Cash Trace, so finance can see the source of a mismatch before close." The business meaning survives deletion of the coined term; the evaluator receives the precise discriminator.

### Worked set

The fixed fictional product is **CloseSignal**, a fictional B2B SaaS product for subscription-company finance teams. All company names, study results, product facts, prices, and performance figures in this worked set are invented for illustration.

The **fixed fact set** does not change between treatments:

CloseSignal connects read-only to the CRM, contract repository, billing system, and general ledger. Each night it matches governing contract terms to generated invoices and resulting ledger entries. Its internal proprietary label for this process is **Contract-to-Cash Trace**. It flags mismatches above $100 before month-end close and never writes back into source systems. In a fictional eight-week pilot across 24 fictional SaaS businesses, it identified a median 0.9 percent discrepancy between contracted and invoiced recurring revenue and reduced manual reconciliation by a fictional median 12 staff-hours per month. A fictional 14-day read-only audit is available without changing production systems.

For the worked set, **market sophistication is deliberately held constant at S4**, a mature market in which competing vendors already talk about automation, AI reconciliation, revenue assurance, anomaly detection, and proprietary matching engines. Only individual awareness changes. This isolates the mechanism-naming decision.

Every treatment below is **D | PI** as a routing prescription. The annotations identify the A/B evidence that constrains the inference.

**Unaware reader, S4 sophisticated market**

**Hero headline**

> Revenue can go missing after the contract is signed and before anyone notices.

**Opening paragraph**

> A renewal changes in the CRM. A billing rule stays old. A discount appears in one system but not another. Each record can look reasonable on its own, so the mismatch often survives until someone is reconciling invoices at month-end. CloseSignal checks those handoffs each night and shows finance where the numbers stop agreeing.

**CTA**

> See the four handoffs where revenue slips

**Annotation:** The audience does not yet have a stable problem or category label, even though the market itself is mature. The opening therefore uses familiar objects: contract, CRM, billing rule, discount, invoice, month-end. It omits "revenue assurance," "reconciliation engine," and "Contract-to-Cash Trace." The mechanism is gestured at with "checks those handoffs each night." The CTA asks for recognition and education, not a demo.

**[Grade D | PI] Routing rationale:** Naming Contract-to-Cash Trace in the hero would force the reader to learn the seller's vocabulary before understanding why the process matters. Adjacent **B-grade** evidence shows unfamiliar jargon reduces processing fluency, even with definitions. ([Shulman et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X20902177)) **Boundary from the B evidence:** If this exact term had already become familiar category language to the audience, it would no longer impose the same unfamiliar-jargon cost.

Notice what has *not* changed. The product still uses the same mechanism. Precision has not been discarded. It has been delayed until the reader possesses the concept that the precision qualifies.

**Problem-aware reader, S4 sophisticated market**

**Hero headline**

> Stop discovering contract-to-invoice mismatches at month-end.

**Opening paragraph**

> CloseSignal checks the contract terms, invoice rules, and ledger entries that finance teams normally reconcile after something looks wrong. It follows each billed amount back through those records every night, flags the exact break, and leaves every source system untouched.

**CTA**

> See a two-minute mismatch walkthrough

**Annotation:** The reader already recognizes the mismatch problem. The opening can therefore name the problem directly and move one step closer to process. The mechanism is still described functionally rather than introduced as a branded noun. "It follows each billed amount back through those records" gives the causal model before asking the reader to remember terminology.

**[Grade D | PI] Routing rationale:** At problem-aware state, the important novelty is not the label. It is the explanation of why this approach finds errors earlier than ordinary reconciliation. The branded mechanism remains one cognitive step too early for the opening. Adjacent **B-grade** jargon evidence supports the vocabulary constraint.

The proof burden also changes. An unaware reader first needs to believe the phenomenon exists. This reader already believes that. Proof should move toward showing the approach actually isolates the failure point. The CTA therefore becomes a mechanism demonstration rather than a general educational asset.

**Solution-aware reader, S4 sophisticated market**

**Hero headline**

> Revenue assurance that traces every billed dollar back to the contract that governs it.

**Opening paragraph**

> CloseSignal uses Contract-to-Cash Trace to match contract terms, invoice records, and ledger entries every night. Instead of scoring an invoice as merely "unusual," it shows which governing term no longer agrees with what was billed. In a fictional eight-week pilot across 24 SaaS companies, the system found a median 0.9 percent gap between contracted and invoiced recurring revenue while removing a fictional 12 hours of monthly manual reconciliation.

**CTA**

> Compare the four reconciliation checks

**Annotation:** The reader knows the solution class, so the category term "revenue assurance" is now useful rather than educational debt. The opening names the mechanism because the reader is equipped to interpret a differentiating "how." Crucially, the name is not allowed to stand alone. The next clause translates it into known comparison objects: contract terms, invoices, ledger entries.

The distinction from competing "anomaly detection" is now central. This buyer does not need another explanation that mismatches are bad. The buyer needs a criterion for comparing approaches.

**[Grade D | PI] Routing rationale:** This is the first state in the worked set where early mechanism naming earns its cost. The direct evidence does not prove that the coined term raises conversion. Persuasion-knowledge evidence does support the need to give a sophisticated reader substance beyond another generic promotional claim, and jargon evidence still requires the mechanism name to be immediately translated. ([Campbell and Kirmani](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/27/1/69/1791556); [Shulman et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X20902177))

The proof also becomes comparative. "We detect discrepancies" is weak in an S4 market because competitors already say equivalent things. "We trace the billed value back to the governing term rather than only scoring the invoice as anomalous" creates an inspectable comparison criterion.

**Product-aware reader, S4 sophisticated market**

**Hero headline**

> See what CloseSignal's Contract-to-Cash Trace catches before your next close.

**Opening paragraph**

> You already know CloseSignal checks contracts, invoices, and ledger entries. The question is whether the trace produces evidence your team can act on. In the fictional eight-week pilot, CloseSignal identified a median 0.9 percent contracted-to-invoiced recurring-revenue discrepancy and removed a fictional 12 reconciliation hours per month. The 14-day audit is read-only and makes no changes to CRM, billing, or ledger data.

**CTA**

> Run the 14-day read-only audit

**Annotation:** Product awareness removes the need to explain what CloseSignal is. The product and mechanism can appear in the headline because recognition is already present. The opening spends almost no space on category education. It answers the unresolved product-aware objections: does it produce actionable evidence, is the result material, and can evaluation be done safely?

**[Grade D | PI] Routing rationale:** The mechanism name now reduces retrieval and comparison cost rather than adding vocabulary cost. The CTA becomes high commitment relative to earlier states, but still matches an evaluation task rather than demanding a commercial contract.

This is also where overexplaining the market becomes costly. Wood and Lynch's **A-grade** evidence shows that higher-knowledge consumers can pay less attention to new product information when prior knowledge creates complacency. ([Wood and Lynch](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/29/3/416/1800953)) **Boundary:** That experiment does not prove that a short hero converts better. It establishes a reason not to bury genuinely new product information beneath redundant category explanation.

**Most-aware reader, S4 sophisticated market**

**Hero headline**

> Run your 14-day CloseSignal audit before this month's close.

**Opening paragraph**

> The audit is read-only, requires no production writeback, and checks the same contract, invoice, and ledger relationships used by Contract-to-Cash Trace. Your team receives the flagged mismatches and their source records before deciding whether to continue. The fictional pilot benchmark was a median 0.9 percent contracted-to-invoiced recurring-revenue discrepancy and 12 reconciliation hours removed per month.

**CTA**

> Start the read-only audit

**Annotation:** The mechanism has moved *down* again. This is the important counterexample to "more sophisticated means name the mechanism more prominently." The reader already knows the product and mechanism. Repeating the explanatory story delays the transaction. The opening leads with timing, operational risk, reversibility, and the evaluation deliverable.

**[Grade D | PI] Routing rationale:** Most-aware treatment is an offer-friction problem, not a vocabulary problem. Mechanism precision remains available as reassurance but no longer deserves the first sentence unless it is the buyer's final unresolved objection.

The five versions expose the core staging rule:

| State | What changed despite identical facts and identical S4 market sophistication | Mechanism treatment | CTA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Lived symptom and handoff failure became the story. | Omitted by name. | Educational. |
| Problem aware | Problem name and causal process became explicit. | Gestured. | Demonstration. |
| Solution aware | Category and competitive discriminator became central. | Named and translated. | Comparison. |
| Product aware | Product-specific evidence, safety, and trial fit became central. | Named prominently. | Evaluation. |
| Most aware | Offer, timing, reversibility, and action became central. | Secondary again. | Start. |

**[Grade D | PI] Rule:** *Mechanism prominence is not monotonic with sophistication.* It can rise from unaware through active comparison and then fall again once the reader already knows the mechanism and is deciding whether to act.

**[Grade D | PI] Rule:** *The same true fact should occupy different rhetorical positions at different awareness states.* The fictional pilot result belongs below problem recognition for the unaware reader, near the differentiation argument for the solution-aware reader, and near the opening for the product-aware buyer.

**[Grade D | PI] Rule:** *A coined mechanism term earns its place when it compresses already meaningful distinctions.* It loses its place when the term itself becomes an additional concept the reader must decode.

**[Grade B] Constraint:** When the term is unfamiliar jargon, there is direct experimental evidence for a processing-fluency cost. ([Shulman et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X20902177)) **Boundary:** The constraint does not apply in the same way when the audience already uses the term or the term is transparently descriptive.

**[Grade A] Constraint:** When the product itself lacks an established cognitive slot, a familiar reference category or well-mapped analogy can improve understanding. ([Moreau et al.](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/27/4/489/1810433); [Roehm and Sternthal](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/28/2/257/1899200)) **Boundary:** Misleading category transfer and hard-to-map analogies reverse the benefit.

### Folklore appendix

| Widely repeated rule | Grade | Earliest traceable lineage found | Adjudication |
|---|---:|---|---|
| "The five stages of awareness are a proven framework." | D | Eugene Schwartz's practitioner framework in *Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966. | False as stated. Useful practitioner model, no controlled validation of the exact five-stage taxonomy found in this review. |
| "Buyers are 70 percent through the purchase before contacting sales." | D | Mutation of several different B2B statistics beginning with CEB/Google's 57 percent and SiriusDecisions' separate 67 percent "done digitally" figure. | False as a universal empirical rule and misstates the provenance. |
| "You must create a new category to win." | D | Modern strong form prominently codified in *Play Bigger*, 2016. | Unsupported universal prescription. Category framing has controlled cognitive evidence; mandatory corporate category creation does not. |
| "Nobody reads long copy anymore." | D | The objection predates digital advertising; a Neil French print ad titled "Nobody reads long copy any more" is documented no later than 2002, and Ogilvy had already been rebutting the underlying belief decades earlier. | False as a universal rule. People do scroll and read relevant material, but attention is strongly concentrated near the top; copy length has no context-free optimum. |

**"The five stages of awareness are a proven framework." [Grade D]**

The traceable source is Schwartz's 1966 practitioner book. Its chapter structure clearly establishes the awareness continuum as a copywriting device. ([chapter record](https://spdrdng.com/posts/summary-of-breakthrough-advertising-by-eugene-m-schwartz)) What does not exist in the reviewed evidence is a controlled program demonstrating that consumers partition uniquely into those five states, that the states are exhaustive, that they are traversed sequentially, or that Schwartz-matched copy outperforms alternatives across the five cells.

Academic consumer research supports a narrower proposition: prior knowledge and decision phase affect processing and information search. Bettman and Park provide B-grade support for that proposition, while Wood and Lynch provide A-grade evidence that prior knowledge can alter attention to new product information. ([Bettman and Park](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/7/3/234/1820883); [Wood and Lynch](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/29/3/416/1800953)) That evidence does not turn the five Schwartz labels into an experimentally proven framework.

**"Buyers are 70 percent through the purchase before contacting sales." [Grade D]**

The provenance matters because three different claims were gradually collapsed into one.

First, Google and CEB published **57 percent**, not 70 percent. Their February 2013 "B2B's Digital Evolution" article states that business buyers did not contact suppliers directly until an average of 57 percent of the purchase process was complete. It says Google and CEB's Marketing Leadership Council worked during 2011 and 2012 with a survey of 1,500 business leaders involved in major purchases for 22 large B2B organizations. The report also describes interviews with leaders at 50 B2B marketing organizations and other supporting research. ([CEB/Google 2013 PDF](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/3928/b2b-digital-evolution_articles_1.pdf))

Second, SiriusDecisions circulated a different 2013 statement: **"67% of the buyer's journey is now done digitally."** "Done digitally" is not the same proposition as "completed before talking to sales." Contemporary accounts of SiriusDecisions' 2015 Summit report that the firm explicitly pushed back on the latter interpretation and said buyers interact with providers in different ways throughout the decision process. ([Demand Gen Report, 2015](https://www.demandgenreport.com/industry-news/siriusdecisions-summit-2015-b2b-buying-mythology-debunked/3252/)) A contemporaneous account from the summit likewise records the clarification that 67 percent concerned digital content consumption, while sales interaction could occur across stages.

Third, the number drifted upward in downstream marketing. A documented 2019 Pardot formulation said **"70% of the buyer's journey is complete before a buyer even reaches out to sales"** and attributed that proposition to SiriusDecisions, even though the Sirius statistic being propagated was originally 67 percent of activity "done digitally." ([provenance discussion preserving the Pardot wording](https://win-loss.agency/post/myth-67-of-the-buyers-journey-is-complete/))

The provenance chain is therefore:

> **CEB/Google: 57% before supplier contact** -> **SiriusDecisions: 67% of journey done digitally** -> **market retelling: 67% before sales** -> **later retelling: 70% before sales**.

Those statements are not interchangeable.

Current field research also demonstrates why a universal fixed percentage is untenable. In 6sense's 2025 B2B survey, reported first seller contact occurred around 61 percent of the journey on average, compared with 69 percent in its 2024 study, within that research program's own methodology. ([6sense 2025](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/)) The value varies by study, sample, definition, and measurement method. No defensible craft rule should be built around "70 percent."

**"You must create a new category to win." [Grade D]**

The strongest modern lineage found in this research is the 2016 book *Play Bigger*, which argues that companies should deliberately design categories and seek category dominance. ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Play_Bigger.html?id=xC89CgAAQBAJ)) The practitioner doctrine has generated extensive case discussion, but retrospective examples of successful category creators do not establish that category creation caused their success or that firms using existing categories cannot win.

Controlled consumer research establishes something narrower and more useful: category labels affect how people understand genuinely novel products, and familiar reference structures can be cognitively useful. ([Moreau, Markman, and Lehmann](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/27/4/489/1810433)) The evidence therefore supports deliberate **category framing**, not compulsory **category invention**.

**"Nobody reads long copy anymore." [Grade D]**

The rule is not traceable to a credible experimental finding. The objection is old advertising folklore. A Neil French advertisement explicitly titled "Nobody reads long copy any more" was already being discussed in the trade press in 2002. ([Strategy, 2002](https://strategyonline.ca/2002/02/25/longcopy-20020225/)) David Ogilvy had likewise rebutted the general "people do not read long copy" belief decades earlier, so the objection itself predates the web. The exact first utterance is not traceable from a primary source in the reviewed record.

Web-use evidence refutes the absolute form without proving the opposite absolute. Nielsen Norman Group's longitudinal usability work reports that users learned to scroll as long web pages became commonplace; by 1997 most users in its studies were scrolling. ([NN/g historical review](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/changes-in-web-usability-since-1994/)) Later eye-tracking work also found a steep attention gradient: in its 2010 study, about 80 percent of viewing time was spent above the fold and 20 percent below it. ([NN/g original scrolling study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention-original-research/)) NN/g's later guidance states the correct interaction principle directly: users do scroll, but what they see first must give them reason to continue. ([page-fold research](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/))

**[Grade C] Rule: Put the highest-value relevance signal early because attention is top-heavy, not because readers categorically refuse long pages.** Longitudinal usability and eye-tracking evidence support top-of-page priority and continued scrolling when the page earns it. ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention-original-research/)) **Boundary:** This is usability research, not a controlled universal test of long versus short sales copy, and attention distribution varies with task, device, design, and reader motivation.

The defensible replacement for all four folklore rules is not another universal slogan. It is the evidence-graded routing model above: awareness determines the conceptual starting point; sophistication determines the competitive and persuasion burden; existing vocabulary determines the learning cost of mechanism and category names; the decision task determines required proof and CTA commitment. The exact integrated routing model remains **D | PI** because that combination has not been directly tested, while its constraints range from B-grade jargon evidence to A-grade experiments on prior knowledge, persuasion knowledge, choice overload, categorization, and analogy.
