# Reader vocabulary, curse of knowledge, and jargon

## Reader vocabulary, curse of knowledge, and jargon

### The curse of knowledge

The failure is not factual inaccuracy. It is perspective failure. Once a writer knows what a product is, how it works, what its internal components are called, and which distinctions matter to the builders, that knowledge becomes difficult to subtract when estimating what a reader knows. The cognitive literature supports this effect across economic judgment, hindsight, expertise, and knowledge-prediction tasks. It does not support the stronger folklore claim that experts are universally bad communicators.

**Rule [Grade A]: Do not use the writer's effortless recognition of a term as evidence that the reader owns it. Require independent evidence of reader familiarity before drafting.** Sources: [Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber, 1989](https://doi.org/10.1086/261651); [Nickerson, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.737); [Tullis and Feder, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9794110/). **Boundary:** the rule weakens when writer and reader demonstrably share the same domain, role, task, and vocabulary. Shared expertise can make technical terminology the more fluent and precise option.

The term "curse of knowledge" entered the experimental literature through Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber. Better-informed participants failed to reproduce less-informed judgments even when doing so served their economic interests. In market experiments, market interaction cut the measured bias by about 50 percent but did not eliminate it. The important point for copy is structural: knowing additional information changes the baseline from which the writer simulates the uninformed reader.

The effect is adjacent to hindsight bias, not identical to it. Fischhoff's classic outcome-knowledge experiments gave participants historical scenarios with or without knowledge of the outcome. Across 24 comparisons, outcome knowledge raised the judged prior probability of the reported outcome every time; the mean increase was 10.8 percentage points, with individual increases ranging from 3.6 to 23.4 points. Once the outcome was known, participants could not fully reconstruct the uncertainty that existed beforehand. Source: [Fischhoff, 1975](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.1.3.288).

**Rule [Grade A]: Treat "this is obvious once explained" as positive evidence of contamination, not evidence of reader familiarity.** Hindsight and knowledge-state research show that possession of the answer changes reconstruction of the prior state. **Boundary:** the inference does not establish that every familiar term is incomprehensible to a novice. It establishes that the writer's introspection cannot settle the question. External reader evidence still can.

Hinds tested the closely related "curse of expertise." Experts, intermediate users, and novices estimated how long novices would need to perform unfamiliar tasks. More expertise produced worse predictions of novice completion time. In one study, novice completion times ranged from 3 to 27 minutes with a median of 12.27 minutes, while predictions across conditions averaged 10.7 minutes. Debiasing prompts improved intermediate users' estimates by about 20 percent but made expert estimates about 2 percent worse. Hinds concluded that experts' superior knowledge interfered with their estimates of novice performance. Source: [Hinds, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.5.2.205).

Tullis and Feder provide more recent evidence. Across four experiments, participants learned trivia facts and estimated what novices would know. Learning the answers impaired both calibration and resolution of those estimates. Experimentally reducing reliance on one's own knowledge did not restore accuracy. This matters operationally because "try to imagine being a novice" is not a sufficient correction. Source: [Tullis and Feder, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9794110/).

**Rule [Grade A]: Perspective-taking alone is not the pre-draft control. Replace introspection with evidence about actual reader language.** **Boundary:** direct audience research can itself be unrepresentative, stale, or contaminated by prompting. The evidence must come from the intended buyer population and task.

The famous tapping demonstration belongs in the lineage but should not carry the rule. Elizabeth Newton's 1990 Stanford dissertation is reported as having tappers predict about 50 percent recognition while listeners identified 3 of 120 tapped songs, or 2.5 percent. The exact figures are repeatedly reproduced in secondary accounts, including a University of Arizona account, but an accessible primary dissertation record and a direct modern replication were not located in this research. **Evidence status for using "50 percent versus 2.5 percent" as an authority claim: Grade D.** It is a memorable illustration, not the evidentiary foundation.

Replication evidence also argues against presenting "the curse" as one giant universal effect. Ryskin and Brown-Schmidt ran seven experiments with 3,074 participants on a false-belief perspective-taking paradigm. Their combined knowledge effect was about \(d=.20\), against approximately \(d=.47\) in the original study they were revisiting; a related plausibility effect was about \(d=.24\), against approximately \(d=.65\) originally. The effect survived, but at substantially smaller magnitude. Source: [Ryskin and Brown-Schmidt, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0092406).

The translation to product writing is specific. Product knowledge changes four things at once: the writer chunks multiple facts into one familiar concept; internal names acquire meaning through repeated exposure; architecture becomes salient because it explains the system to the builder; and the writer underestimates the inferential work required to turn a mechanism into a customer consequence. A phrase such as "policy-aware orchestration layer" can therefore feel shorter than its plain-language translation to the person who has spent six months discussing that layer. To a first-time buyer it contains three unresolved questions: what is being orchestrated, what policy means here, and why either fact matters. This is the communication analogue supported by the expertise, knowledge-imputation, and processing-fluency literatures.

### Why experts produce jargon

Jargon has more than one cause. Treating all jargon as vanity produces bad editing because some technical terms are genuinely efficient. The intervention must follow the cause. Brown, Anicich, and Galinsky explicitly distinguish jargon's coordination value inside a knowledgeable group from its signaling and exclusion costs outside that group. Their nine-study program included experiments, live communication, and an archival analysis of roughly 64,000 dissertations. Source: [Brown, Anicich, and Galinsky, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.07.001).

| Cause | Evidence | Intervention |
|---|---|---|
| **Genuine compression** | Specialized terminology can increase precision and efficiency when interlocutors share its meaning. Brown et al. explicitly note this legitimate function. **Grade A** for the general in-group/out-group distinction. | **Rule [Grade A]: Keep the specialized term when the intended reader demonstrably owns it and the ordinary-language replacement loses a distinction relevant to the decision.** Source: [Brown et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.07.001). **Boundary:** outside that discourse community, the compression disappears because the reader must first decode the term. |
| **Identity and competence signaling** | Jargon can mark membership in a specialized community. The strongest direct causal evidence is status-related rather than a general identity effect: lower-status communicators increased jargon when evaluation concerns were salient. **Grade A** for status signaling; **Grade B** for generalizing that mechanism to all group-identity jargon. | **Rule [Grade A]: Do not retain a term merely because it sounds like the category or signals that the company is sophisticated. Require reader ownership or decision relevance.** Source: [Brown et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.07.001). **Boundary:** signaling group membership is useful in genuinely expert communities where recognized terminology is itself evidence of competent participation. |
| **Insecurity compensation** | Across Brown et al.'s studies, lower status increased jargon, including under experimental manipulation. In one communication-motive test, 70.2 percent of participants choosing a high-jargon title selected an evaluative-concern motive, versus 58.3 percent in the low-jargon condition. Oppenheimer separately found across five experiments that needless lexical complexity reduced perceived intelligence rather than raising it. **Grade A.** | **Rule [Grade A]: When complexity adds no semantic precision, simplify it. Needlessly difficult vocabulary is not a reliable competence signal and can reverse the intended impression.** Sources: [Brown et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.07.001); [Oppenheimer, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1178). **Boundary:** Oppenheimer tested needless complexity, not technical terms that are the conventional precise names for a concept. |
| **Copy-paste from internal artifacts** | Direct controlled evidence that tickets, architecture diagrams, specs, or decks mechanically cause jargon-heavy marketing copy was not located. Practitioner usability research repeatedly identifies "maker-centric" and feature-driven language as a real content failure, but it does not isolate copy-paste provenance as the causal mechanism. **Grade C** for the maker-centric failure; **Grade D** for literal artifact leakage as a quantified cause. | **Rule [Grade D]: Treat vocabulary copied from specs, tickets, architecture diagrams, internal project names, and engineering decks as untrusted until separately admitted by the reader-language test.** This is a house control, not a measured threshold. |

Brown et al.'s most important result for expert copy is that jargon is not always produced because the communicator thinks it is clearer. Evaluation concern independently increases it. In their experimental program, status manipulations increased jargon in settings where the communication itself was being judged. That gives a writing system a concrete diagnostic: when a sophisticated term has no precision advantage, test whether it is functioning as a badge rather than a handle.

Oppenheimer supplies the reader-side correction. In Experiments 1 through 3, increasing needless textual complexity lowered judged intelligence. Experiment 4 reproduced the direction by making text perceptually harder to read. Experiment 5 showed an important boundary: when readers could attribute difficulty to an irrelevant external cause, they discounted the fluency cue and could overcorrect. Source: [Oppenheimer, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1178).

**Rule [Grade A]: Replace complexity that exists only to impress. Do not replace complexity that carries necessary domain information.** **Boundary:** perceived fluency is attributional. When difficulty is expected, externally caused, or diagnostic of genuine expertise, readers need not interpret it as low competence.

The strongest direct jargon experiments reinforce the distinction. Shulman and colleagues tested 650 participants and found that jargon reduced processing fluency and scientific engagement even when definitions were supplied. Bullock and colleagues separately found jargon affected reactions to scientific technologies through metacognitive difficulty, not simply failures to understand literal definitions. Sources: [Shulman et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X20902177); [Bullock et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963662519865687).

**Rule [Grade A]: A parenthetical definition does not automatically neutralize an unnecessary unfamiliar term. If the reader does not need the label later, translate the idea and omit the label.** **Boundary:** a definition is appropriate when the label itself must be learned for a later action, comparison, compliance obligation, technical discussion, or purchase decision.

### The reader knowledge boundary and when a mechanism name helps

The useful distinction is not "jargon versus plain English." It is **reader-owned vocabulary versus vocabulary that imposes a learning transaction**. Jargon is audience-relative. A technical term can be the easiest phrase on the page for an expert and the hardest for a novice. NN/g's usability guidance expresses the same principle operationally: interfaces and content should use concepts and language familiar to the user rather than internal system vocabulary.

Processing fluency explains part of the cost. Fluency research shows that ease of processing affects a wide range of judgments, including familiarity, truth, confidence, liking, and evaluations of the communicator. It is a metacognitive cue, not a truth detector. Source: [Alter and Oppenheimer, 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1088868309341564).

**Rule [Grade A]: For a nonexpert audience, default to the highest-familiarity wording that preserves the decision-relevant meaning.** **Boundary:** familiar wording loses its advantage when the established technical term is itself more recognizable to the target reader than the proposed paraphrase, or when simplification removes a distinction the reader needs.

Fluency is not equivalent to "make everything maximally easy." Repetition-induced fluency can increase perceived truth even without corresponding evidence, which makes fluency a source of bias as well as comprehension ease. Claims that perceptual disfluency reliably improves learning have also failed repeatedly. A 120-participant study of Sans Forgetica found no learning benefit from the disfluent font, while self-testing did improve recall; later work describes repeated failures to obtain a robust general disfluency benefit.

**Rule [Grade A]: Do not deliberately make customer copy harder to process in order to trigger "deeper thinking."** Sources: [Wetzler et al., 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440211056624) and the replication literature summarized in [Weissgerber et al., 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11409-023-09366-7). **Boundary:** this rule concerns perceptual or linguistic difficulty. Useful decision friction, such as requiring confirmation before an irreversible financial action, is a different intervention.

#### The three vocabulary classes

For production purposes, classify terms before drafting:

| Class | Definition | Drafting treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Reader owns it** | The intended audience already uses or reliably recognizes the term in the intended meaning. | Use it without explanation unless ambiguity remains. |
| **Reader will accept being taught it** | The reader does not reliably own the term, but the term names something the reader must distinguish, compare, search for, configure, discuss, or remember. | Teach once at the point of need, attach immediately to a known consequence, then reuse consistently. |
| **Reader will not pay for it** | The term is unfamiliar and learning it creates no downstream decision, action, retrieval, comparison, compliance, or memory benefit for the reader. | Translate or delete it. |

**Rule [Grade A]: Familiarity must be audience-specific, not population-wide.** **Boundary:** familiarity data from one buyer role cannot be transferred automatically to another. "Kubernetes" can be reader-owned for a platform engineer and reader-unowned for a procurement executive evaluating the same product.

#### What counts as evidence that the reader owns a term

Use reader-origin evidence in descending order of strength: unaided audience testing; actual search queries; repeated spontaneous use in sales calls, support conversations, reviews, community discussions, or interviews; established category terminology used by the same buyer role. Google Search Console directly exposes the queries through which users encountered a site, making search terms observable behavior rather than writer intuition.

**Rule [Grade C]: Treat a term that repeatedly appears unprompted in reader-origin language as stronger evidence of ownership than a term that appears only in competitor copy or internal documents.** Sources: Janelle Estes's user-centric language guidance at [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-centric-language/) and Google's [Search Console query documentation](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7576553?hl=en). **Boundary:** customers can repeat language they have just learned from vendors, and search-query data contain exploratory terms from people outside the target segment. Reader-source provenance still matters.

No controlled study establishes a universal frequency such as "five customers used the word, therefore the audience owns it." Any such count is a house threshold.

**Operational ownership threshold [Grade D]:** for automated pre-draft classification, call a term "reader-owned" when at least one of these conditions is met: it occurs spontaneously in at least 10 independent reader-origin artifacts from the target role; it appears in at least 20 independent target-audience search queries; or an unaided vocabulary test shows at least 80 percent of sampled target readers select the intended meaning. These values are deliberately conservative house defaults. They are not research-derived cutoffs.

#### Novel proper nouns

There is no direct evidence that introducing one unfamiliar proprietary name into customer copy causes a specific probability that the reader will learn it, ignore it, or leave. That three-way behavioral claim has not been measured well enough to support a universal number.

Indirect evidence shows that the form of an unfamiliar name can change judgment. Experiments on difficult-to-pronounce names find effects on perceived unfamiliarity, control, risk, and preference. Leonhardt and colleagues found difficult-to-pronounce product names could make products seem less controllable and reduce preference when controllability mattered. Related fluency work has found pronunciation difficulty can change risk judgments, although later research demonstrates that direction depends on category expectations.

**Rule [Grade B]: Treat every new proprietary noun as a learning cost. Introduce it only when the reader needs the name later.** Source: [Leonhardt et al., 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597821000480). **Boundary:** a new name can itself create useful distinctiveness, and difficult or novel naming can sometimes signal novelty or risk in a direction that fits the category. The evidence does not justify "easy name always wins."

Learning research also rejects the assumption that one exposure equals acquisition. Repeated exposure reliably contributes to vocabulary learning, with dose and spacing affecting outcomes. Those studies come largely from educational contexts, so they establish the direction, not a marketing-copy repetition count.

**Rule [Grade B]: Do not coin a mechanism name, define it once, and assume it has become reader-owned.** **Boundary:** a name already familiar from category search, peer discussion, standards, or previous buying experience does not begin at zero knowledge.

#### When the mechanism name should survive

The counter-rule is as important as the jargon warning. Technical language can increase credibility when it matches the expected audience. In a randomized experiment with 98 participants, Zimmermann and Jucks manipulated both medical technicality and the stated audience of an online forum. Credibility was higher when technicality matched the designated audience; the interaction had partial eta squared of about .031. Source: [Zimmermann and Jucks, 2018](https://www.jmir.org/2018/1/e30/).

**Rule [Grade B]: A mechanism name earns its place when it is either reader-owned or decision-required.** Source: [Zimmermann and Jucks, 2018](https://www.jmir.org/2018/1/e30/). **Boundary:** technicality that merely matches the seller's sophistication but not the reader's produces the opposite accommodation problem.

Use this decision rule:

> **Mechanism name = differentiator** when the term names a real causal, architectural, regulatory, or technical difference that the intended buyer uses to compare options, search, specify requirements, configure the product, assess risk, or defend the purchase.
>
> **Methodology label = obstruction** when the term merely gives a proprietary name to the seller's internal way of producing an outcome the reader can already understand without learning the label.

This composite rule is **Grade B for audience-technicality matching, Grade C for search-language matching, and Grade D for competitive-density as an independent trigger.** Buyer sophistication is not enough by itself. Competitive density is not enough by itself. The existence of an established comparison vocabulary and actual reader search behavior are stronger indicators.

A technical reader is therefore not automatically a license for architecture language. The relevant question is whether that architecture distinction participates in the reader's decision. A database engineer comparing consistency models can need "serializable isolation." The same engineer buying payroll software does not need the payroll vendor's "Adaptive Ledger Reconciliation Mesh" unless that mechanism changes an outcome or constraint the engineer must evaluate.

**Rule [Grade B]: Attach an unfamiliar mechanism name to its consequence at first mention: "[name] is [plain mechanism], so [reader-relevant consequence]."** The evidence supports the need to preserve processing fluency and audience accommodation, not that exact sentence template. The template itself is **Grade D**. **Boundary:** do not add the explanation when the term is demonstrably reader-owned; redundant definitions can signal that the material is below an expert reader's level.

### Concreteness and translation discipline

Concrete language is not synonymous with short words. It names observable actors, actions, objects, quantities, events, and consequences. Abstract language packages events into categories such as "optimization," "enablement," "transformation," "governance," and "orchestration." Some abstraction is essential because abstraction compresses recurring patterns. The failure occurs when the abstraction replaces the event the reader needs to picture.

Packard and Berger combined field data with experiments in customer-service settings. Increasing linguistic concreteness by one standard deviation was associated with roughly 9 percent higher customer satisfaction and at least 13 percent more subsequent spending; experiments supported perceived listening and attention as mechanisms. Source: [Packard and Berger, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucab012).

**Rule [Grade A]: When two formulations are equally accurate, prefer the one that lets the reader identify who does what to what and what changes as a result.** Source: [Packard and Berger, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucab012). **Boundary:** abstract language is superior when the reader needs to reason about a category, policy, class of events, repeated process, or higher-level relationship rather than a single concrete instance.

Do not inflate this into "concrete language always creates trust." Experimental work linking concreteness with truth judgments has produced failed or weakened replications. The robust commercial finding is narrower: in service interactions, concrete language can signal attention to the customer's specific situation and improve customer outcomes.

#### Nominalization and agency

The common editing instruction "replace nominalizations with verbs" is too strong. Laura Winther Balling directly tested several pieces of professional writing advice, including nominalizations versus sentential constructions and passive versus active alternatives. Twenty-seven students read manipulated authentic texts while eye movements were measured. The supposedly problematic constructions did not independently produce worse comprehension; cohesion and coherence were more important. Source: [Balling, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0047281617696983).

**Rule [Grade B]: Treat nominalization as a diagnostic flag, not an error. Rewrite it when it suppresses a decision-relevant actor, action, object, or causal relationship.** Source: [Balling, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0047281617696983). **Boundary:** retain nominalizations when they provide useful cohesion, name an established technical or legal concept, or let a passage refer compactly to a process already explained.

Agency does affect interpretation. Fausey and Boroditsky experimentally varied agentive and nonagentive descriptions of accidents. Subtle wording altered blame and financial-liability judgments. Other work in this line found language patterns affected memory for causal agents. Source: [Fausey and Boroditsky, 2010](https://doi.org/10.3758/PBR.17.5.644).

**Rule [Grade B]: Name the actor when responsibility, control, causality, authorization, or accountability matters to the reader.** Source: [Fausey and Boroditsky, 2010](https://doi.org/10.3758/PBR.17.5.644). **Boundary:** omit the actor when it is genuinely unknown, immaterial, already established, or when the process or object is deliberately the discourse focus.

For an automated writing system, useful measurable markers are therefore **flags, not universal limits**:

| Marker | What the system can count | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Reader-unowned nouns and verbs | Terms failing the audience-ownership test | **Grade A** as a risk dimension; no validated universal count. |
| Nominalizations | Derived nouns replacing plausible actions, such as `-tion`, `-ment`, `-ance`, `-ence`, `-al` forms | **Grade B** as a review flag; **Grade D** for any fixed density threshold. |
| Agentless passives | Passive constructions without an expressed responsible actor | **Grade B** when agency matters; not a universal readability defect. |
| Concrete actor-action-object triples | Explicit responsible noun + finite action verb + affected object/result | **Grade A** as a preferred pattern when equally accurate; no minimum percentage is validated. |
| Proprietary proper nouns | Capitalized or coined names not in the reader corpus | **Grade B** as a learning-cost flag; **Grade D** for a universal maximum. |
| Lexical familiarity | Corpus frequency plus target-reader use | **Grade A** for the general familiarity effect; target-corpus selection remains audience-specific. |

#### Translation discipline

"Voice of customer" is useful only when treated as evidence about **how customers conceptualize problems**, not as a source of product truth. Customer language can contain misconceptions, folk causal theories, outdated category terms, or descriptions that are legally or technically inaccurate.

**Rule [Grade C]: Mine customer language for problem statements, desired outcomes, objections, comparisons, verbs, and category vocabulary. Do not copy customer claims about the product's mechanism until those claims are verified against the fact set.** Sources: Janelle Estes at [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-centric-language/) and Ordenes et al.'s text-mining work on customer-experience feedback. **Boundary:** in regulated, medical, financial, scientific, and safety contexts, accuracy overrides mimicry.

| Method | What it produces | Evidence | Limit and naive failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Voice-of-customer interviews** | The reader's spontaneous names for jobs, pains, outcomes, constraints, comparisons, and objections | **Grade C.** NN/g documents user-centric language as a usability practice. [Source](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-centric-language/). | Interviewer wording primes vocabulary. Stated language is not automatically the language used during an actual purchase. |
| **Review and support-ticket mining** | High-frequency problem descriptions, failure language, task verbs, emotional vocabulary, and recurring category terms | **Grade B** for structured text-mining methods; **Grade C** for using them specifically as copy vocabulary. [Ordenes et al., 2014](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/analyzing-customer-experience-feedback-using-text-mining-a-lingui/). | Tickets oversample failures and existing customers. Reviews oversample unusually happy or unhappy users. Frequency does not establish factual correctness. |
| **Sales-call language capture** | Live buying vocabulary: requirements, objections, competitor names, category criteria, words buyers use before being trained by the seller | **Grade C** as a practitioner user-research method. [NN/g user-centric language guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-centric-language/). | Seller questions can seed terms. Later portions of calls are contaminated by vocabulary introduced by the salesperson. Mine buyer language before the seller explains the product separately from language after explanation. |
| **Search-query language** | Words buyers type before reaching the vendor: category names, problem formulations, feature terms, competitor comparisons | **Grade C** as a content-research method using directly observed behavior. [Google Search Console documentation](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7576553?hl=en). | Search data exclude needs expressed elsewhere, contain irrelevant audiences, and are shaped by current ranking. A query proves that someone used a term, not that every buyer owns it. |
| **Message testing** | Direct evidence about comprehension, preference, credibility, recall, task success, or conversion for alternative formulations | **Grade C** for practitioner content testing with documented methods. [NN/g content-testing guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/testing-content-websites/). | Preference tests without comprehension or behavior measures reward surface taste. A winning variant does not reveal which linguistic mechanism caused the win. |

**Rule [Grade C]: Preserve provenance. Every harvested phrase should retain source type, speaker role, date, stage of journey, and whether the phrase occurred before or after product explanation.** This follows from the documented differences among observed search behavior, support feedback, and user-centric research methods. **Boundary:** provenance does not establish representativeness; a corpus can be perfectly tagged and still sample the wrong people.

### Operational pre-draft test

This test runs **before generation**. It operates on the fact set and audience evidence, not on a completed draft.

**Rule [Grade A]: The system must resolve reader vocabulary before selecting headline, body-copy, or CTA language.** The grade rests on the curse-of-knowledge, audience-familiarity, jargon, and fluency literatures. **Boundary:** when no audience evidence exists, the resulting classifications are house defaults and must be marked as such rather than inferred from the product specification.

#### Input objects

The procedure takes five machine-readable inputs:

1. `FACT_SET`: verified claims, mechanisms, constraints, entities, actions, outcomes.
2. `AUDIENCE_ROLE`: one explicit buyer/user role for this pass.
3. `READER_CORPUS`: search queries, buyer utterances, support/review language, audience documents.
4. `CATEGORY_LEXICON`: established category terms and standards relevant to that role.
5. `INTERNAL_LEXICON`: project names, architecture labels, ticket vocabulary, engineering names, proprietary methodology names.

The list itself is an operational design, **Grade D**. The requirement to separate reader evidence from internal evidence is supported by the Grade A and C evidence above.

#### Token classification procedure

For every content noun and lexical verb in `FACT_SET`:

**Pass A: normalize.** Lemmatize the token or multiword expression; preserve acronyms and proper nouns separately.

**Pass B: provenance check.** Mark whether the term occurs in `INTERNAL_LEXICON`. Internal occurrence supplies **zero ownership evidence**.

**Pass C: reader-evidence check.** Search `READER_CORPUS` for exact and inflectional matches used with the intended meaning.

**Pass D: category check.** Search `CATEGORY_LEXICON`.

**Pass E: necessity check.** Determine whether removing the term prevents the reader from performing any of these observable downstream tasks: compare options, search for the capability, specify a requirement, configure the product, assess risk, satisfy compliance, explain the purchase, or execute the CTA.

**Pass F: plain-equivalent check.** Determine whether a reader-owned phrase expresses the same decision-relevant meaning without material loss.

Then classify:

```text
IF term meets the reader-ownership threshold:
    CLASS = READER_OWNS_IT

ELSE IF term is necessary for a downstream reader task
        AND no reader-owned equivalent preserves the required distinction:
    CLASS = READER_WILL_ACCEPT_BEING_TAUGHT_IT

ELSE:
    CLASS = READER_WILL_NOT_PAY_FOR_IT
```

The classification architecture is **Grade A** for distinguishing audience familiarity from writer familiarity and **Grade D** for the exact algorithm. **Boundary:** semantic equivalence cannot always be resolved mechanically in legal, medical, financial, scientific, or highly technical claims; the verified fact representation remains authoritative.

#### Deterministic ownership defaults

No research establishes universal corpus-count cutoffs. The following are therefore explicit house defaults, **Grade D**:

```text
READER_OWNS_IT if any one is true:

A. >= 10 independent target-role reader artifacts use the term spontaneously
   in the intended meaning;

B. >= 20 independent target-audience search queries contain the term
   in the intended meaning;

C. >= 80% of a target-reader vocabulary-test sample identify the intended
   meaning without explanation;

D. the term is an externally established standard/category label
   required for the reader's task and the audience is the role that uses it.
```

Do not count company-authored pages, internal decks, generated copy, salesperson language, or text shown to the customer before the customer's use of the term as spontaneous ownership evidence.

#### Drafting treatment by class

**READER_OWNS_IT**

**Rule [Grade A]: Use the reader-owned term directly. Prefer it over an unfamiliar synonym invented to sound simpler.** **Boundary:** if the term has multiple category meanings, disambiguate it.

**READER_WILL_ACCEPT_BEING_TAUGHT_IT**

**Rule [Grade B]: Teach the term at first need, connect it immediately to an owned concept or consequence, and reuse one stable label thereafter.** Sources: jargon and audience-matching experiments. **Boundary:** remove the teaching step when it is redundant for expert readers; remove the term itself when it stops serving a downstream task.

Template, **Grade D**:

```text
[Owned problem/consequence] + [plain mechanism] + [new label only if needed later]
```

Example:

```text
Before:
HelioStack uses DeltaFence isolation for cross-tenant execution.

After:
Each customer's jobs run in a separate execution boundary, called DeltaFence,
so one customer's code cannot access another customer's workspace.
```

The fictional term survives only because the hypothetical reader must later configure or compare `DeltaFence`.

**READER_WILL_NOT_PAY_FOR_IT**

**Rule [Grade A for the unfamiliarity cost, Grade D for automatic deletion]: Translate to reader-owned actor, action, object, and consequence. Delete the original label unless needed for factual traceability.** **Boundary:** do not delete a term merely because it is rare in general English when it is established expert vocabulary for the actual audience.

#### Final pre-draft gate

Generation is blocked until all of the following are true:

| Gate | Requirement | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary provenance | Every content noun and lexical verb has a classification | **D**, operational house control |
| Proprietary names | Every novel proper noun has a documented downstream purpose or is removed | **B** for cost signal, **D** for mandatory gate |
| Internal labels | No internal-only name can enter the draft without passing the same reader test | **C** for maker-centric-language control, **D** for exact gate |
| Mechanisms | Every taught mechanism is attached to the decision consequence that justifies teaching it | **B** |
| Agency | Where responsibility/control matters, an actor is available in the fact representation | **B** |
| Vocabulary uncertainty | Unsupported familiarity assumptions are marked unknown, never silently promoted to reader-owned | **A** |

### Threshold adjudication

#### "Allow at most one unfamiliar concept per sentence"

| Field | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | "Allow at most one unfamiliar concept per sentence." |
| **Verdict** | **No measured basis for the one-concept threshold. Do not present it as cognitive science.** |
| **Grade** | **D** |
| **Evidence** | Working-memory research supports severe capacity limits, but a working-memory "chunk" is not equivalent to an unfamiliar marketing concept. Vocabulary-coverage research shows that unknown vocabulary impairs comprehension, but the relationship is not a universal one-term-per-sentence step function. Hu and Nation's influential L2 work associated very high lexical coverage with adequate unassisted comprehension; later work found a more continuous relationship, and Kremmel et al.'s replication explicitly tested the generality of the claimed 98 percent threshold. [Kremmel et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12622). |
| **Replacement** | **No evidence-backed per-sentence number exists.** Keep "one unfamiliar concept per sentence" only as a **Grade D house guardrail**. A stronger rule is: every reader-unowned term must be either decision-required and taught, or removed. For automated risk scoring, flag any sentence containing two or more reader-unowned content terms for testing, but label that flag **Grade D**. |
| **Boundary** | L2 lexical-coverage findings concern extended reading and vocabulary knowledge, not short commercial copy. They cannot justify importing 95 percent or 98 percent as a universal landing-page target. |

Hu and Nation's original work examined densities of unknown vocabulary in extended texts, and the later replication literature challenges the idea that one fixed coverage percentage cleanly separates comprehensible from incomprehensible text. That evidence supports minimizing reader-unowned vocabulary; it does not support a sentence-level count of one.

#### "Two abstract nouns in one clause is usually one too many; three is a rewrite"

| Field | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | "Two abstract nouns in one clause is usually one too many; three is a rewrite." |
| **Verdict** | **No evidence supports the numbers two or three. Reject them as scientific thresholds.** |
| **Grade** | **D** for the count rule |
| **Evidence** | Balling's controlled eye-tracking experiment found no independent comprehension penalty for nominalizations versus recommended sentential constructions across manipulated authentic texts. The study was small, with 27 readers, so it rejects confidence in a simple count rule rather than proving nominalization never matters. [Balling, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0047281617696983). |
| **Replacement** | **Rule [Grade B]: rewrite an abstraction when it conceals an actor, action, causal relation, or observable consequence needed by the reader. Do not rewrite because a clause crossed a noun count.** |
| **Source for replacement** | [Fausey and Boroditsky, 2010](https://doi.org/10.3758/PBR.17.5.644); [Balling, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0047281617696983). |
| **Boundary** | Nominalizations legitimately create cohesion, name recurring processes, and encode established domain concepts. Legal and technical genres sometimes require them. Corpus work also shows their distribution depends strongly on subject matter. |

A better machine test is not `abstract_noun_count >= 3`. It is:

```text
FOR each abstract or nominalized noun:
    Can the system recover a specific actor?
    Can it recover a finite action?
    Can it recover the object/result?
    Does the reader need that actor/action/result?

IF yes to the last question and one of the first three is missing:
    rewrite candidate
```

The trigger logic is **Grade B** in principle and **Grade D** in this exact implementation.

#### "Prefer these verbs: check, compare, find, show, verify, measure, review, choose, send, create, remove, correct, approve, reject, schedule, calculate"

| Field | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | A fixed whitelist of these verbs improves comprehension. |
| **Verdict** | **No evidence supports this particular set. It is editorial preference.** |
| **Grade** | **D** for the list |
| **Evidence** | Psycholinguistic evidence strongly supports faster and easier processing of more frequent/familiar words, including frequency effects during sentence processing. It does not establish that these 16 verbs form a universally superior set. |
| **Replacement** | **Rule [Grade A]: prefer the highest-frequency reader-owned verb that preserves the factual relation and gives the reader strong information scent about the action.** For CTAs, the action named should predict what happens next. |
| **Supporting sources** | Word-frequency processing research and NN/g's finding that vague CTA verbs reduce information scent. [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/3-ia-mistakes/). |
| **Boundary** | A familiar generic verb can be worse than a more specialized verb when the specialized verb names the exact action. "Process," "run," or "do" is not automatically clearer than "encrypt," "deploy," "reconcile," or "amortize" for a reader who owns the latter term. |

The current list is defensible as a house lexicon of often-useful concrete actions, not as a psycholinguistic standard.

#### "A self-contained passage that survives being quoted alone is two or three sentences"

| Field | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | A quotation-safe self-contained passage is optimally two or three sentences. |
| **Verdict** | **No evidence establishes a two- or three-sentence optimum.** |
| **Grade** | **D** |
| **Evidence** | Discourse-comprehension research treats meaning as dependent on relations across sentences and prior context. Machine-reading work can sometimes extract a small number of evidence sentences without losing answer performance, but the number is task- and document-dependent rather than a universal 2-3 sentence unit. |
| **Replacement** | **No numeric replacement.** Use a semantic self-containment test: the extracted passage must resolve its referents, state the claim, retain the qualification or scope needed to keep the claim true, and include the cause/evidence when that cause/evidence is necessary to interpret the claim. This exact test is **Grade D**, built from discourse requirements rather than a validated passage-length threshold. |
| **Boundary** | Very simple factual claims can be self-contained in one sentence. Qualified causal, comparative, legal, medical, or statistical claims can require more than three. |

A safe production implementation is therefore:

```text
quotation_safe = (
    no_unresolved_pronoun_or_deictic
    AND no_missing_named_subject
    AND claim_scope_preserved
    AND qualification_preserved
    AND comparison_baseline_preserved_if_required
    AND causal_or_evidentiary_context_preserved_if_required
)
```

The implementation is **Grade D**. Keep "two or three sentences" only as a layout preference.

### Folklore appendix

The rules below are widely repeated in plain-language, UX, marketing, and copywriting practice. Their absolute forms do not survive scrutiny.

#### "Write at a grade 8 reading level"

**Folklore claim:** all customer-facing copy should be written at an eighth-grade reading level.

**Verdict: Grade D as a universal rule.**

The traceable scientific lineage is readability measurement, not an experimentally discovered eighth-grade optimum. Flesch's mid-20th-century readability work and the later Flesch-Kincaid grade-level formula created numerical estimates based largely on word and sentence features. Eighth-grade targets became institutional conventions in public and health communication because many materials must serve broad populations with varied literacy. Current U.S. public-health guidance itself states that readability is only a rough indicator and that there is no single best readability level; it gives seventh-to-eighth grade as an average ceiling and lower targets for especially critical information.

**Earliest traceable origin:** no single primary study establishes "grade 8" as the optimum. The measurable lineage begins with readability formulas; the specific eighth-grade target appears as later public-information and health-literacy policy. Modern California guidance, for example, explicitly uses eighth grade or lower as an institutional standard, not as a universal conversion law.

**Where it is true:** eighth grade can be a defensible accessibility ceiling for broad public audiences and can identify obviously difficult drafts. **Where it is false:** expert B2B, legal, technical, scientific, and regulated communication can require vocabulary that inflates formula scores while remaining maximally clear to the target reader. A formula also cannot tell whether the reader knows `OAuth`, `EBITDA`, `arrhythmia`, or `Kubernetes`.

**What the evidence supports [Rule, Grade C]: use readability scores as a coarse alarm, never as the definition of reader comprehension.** Source: [U.S. EPA readability guidance](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely/readability-developing-and-pretesting-concepts-messages-materials). **Boundary:** public-health and government audiences differ from specialist commercial audiences.

#### "Never use jargon"

**Folklore claim:** jargon is always bad.

**Verdict: Grade D as an absolute.**

An early influential plain-style formulation appears in George Orwell's 1946 "Politics and the English Language," which instructs writers to prefer everyday equivalents when available. The modern evidence supports the conditional principle, not the absolute prohibition. Nonexpert readers suffer fluency and engagement costs from unnecessary technical language, including when definitions are supplied. Expert readers can instead interpret familiar specialist vocabulary as appropriate accommodation, and replacing an exact specialist term with an unfamiliar paraphrase can make expert material worse.

**Earliest traceable lineage:** [George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language," 1946](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/) is an early explicit anti-jargon style rule in the modern plain-language tradition. The exact slogan "never use jargon" has no single experimental origin.

**Where it is true:** unnecessary out-group terminology in material for nonexperts. **Where it is false:** expert-to-expert communication, standards, regulated categories, technical comparisons, search terms, and any situation where the specialist term is the reader's shortest precise handle.

**Replacement [Rule, Grade A]: never use reader-unowned jargon without a downstream reason to teach it. Do use reader-owned jargon when it is the clearest precise term.** Sources: [Shulman et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X20902177); [Brown et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.07.001). **Boundary:** ownership must be established for the actual audience, not inferred from professional status alone.

#### "Readability scores predict conversion"

**Folklore claim:** lowering the reading grade or increasing a readability score will increase conversion.

**Verdict: Grade D as a universal causal rule.**

There is observational evidence that textual readability features contain predictive information about landing-page conversion. Korniichuk and Boryczka analyzed a commercial landing-page dataset and built machine-learning models using readability features. The important result is not "simpler always converted better." Correlations were low for prominent readability indices, some readability features related positively and others negatively to conversion, and the same index could have positive and negative effects in different ranges. The authors explicitly distinguished prediction from causal conversion optimization. Source: [Korniichuk and Boryczka, 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/23/11/1388).

**Earliest traceable origin:** marketing claims linking readability formulas directly to conversion appear in practitioner material rather than a founding controlled study. No primary experiment establishing a universal readability-to-conversion function was located.

**Where it is true:** readability features can correlate with conversion and can contribute predictive information in a particular dataset. **Where it is false:** there is no evidence that monotonically lowering reading level will monotonically raise conversion. Very short sentences, low lexical diversity, and high difficulty can all associate with worse outcomes in different regions of the same dataset.

**Replacement [Rule, Grade B]: use readability metrics to identify variants for testing, not to choose the winner without testing.** Source: [Korniichuk and Boryczka, 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/23/11/1388). **Boundary:** the cited study is observational and platform-specific; it does not identify causal effects of rewriting one page while holding every other variable constant.

#### "Customers do not care how it works, only what it does"

**Folklore claim:** mechanism is irrelevant; sell only outcomes.

**Verdict: Grade D as an absolute.**

The rule belongs to the long sales-advice lineage summarized by slogans such as Elmer Wheeler's 1937 "sell the sizzle, not the steak." It captures a real correction to feature-first selling but becomes false when elevated into an audience law. Mechanism affects purchase when it establishes credibility, risk, compatibility, controllability, security, performance, regulatory fit, or a comparison criterion. Technicality matched to an expert audience can raise credibility; technical terms can also be the vocabulary buyers explicitly search and compare.

**Earliest traceable lineage:** Elmer Wheeler's *Tested Sentences That Sell* (1937) popularized the "sizzle" formulation. The modern statement "customers do not care how it works" is a stronger descendant, not an experimentally established law.

**Where it is true:** when the mechanism creates no buyer-relevant distinction and is merely the seller's internal production story. A shopper does not need to learn a retailer's ranking-pipeline architecture to understand that the app finds cheaper flights. **Where it is false:** technical procurement, developer tools, security, medicine, finance, regulated products, industrial equipment, and dense categories in which the mechanism explains why a claimed outcome is credible or materially different.

**Replacement [Rule, Grade B]: lead with the reader's problem or consequence, then name the mechanism when the mechanism changes a criterion the reader must evaluate.** Source: [Zimmermann and Jucks, 2018](https://www.jmir.org/2018/1/e30/). **Boundary:** even sophisticated buyers do not need every true implementation detail. Technical relevance is determined by the decision, not by the amount of technical knowledge available.
